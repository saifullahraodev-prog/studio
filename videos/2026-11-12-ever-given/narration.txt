On the twenty-third of March, twenty twenty-one, one ship turned sideways in the Suez Canal. And for six days, nothing could get past it.

Behind it, hundreds of ships piled up. Oil tankers, car carriers, other giant container ships, and ships full of live animals.

Every single day, about nine point six billion dollars' worth of goods were stuck in that traffic jam.

And the first machine that showed up to dig it out was one small excavator. Next to a ship four hundred metres long.

The whole internet laughed. But behind the jokes was a real problem. Nobody knew how to move a ship that heavy out of a sandbank that tight. And some experts said it could take weeks.

So how did one ship get stuck so badly? How did they finally get it out? And why was it then held prisoner for more than three months?

The ship is called Ever Given. It was built in Japan by Imabari Shipbuilding and finished in twenty eighteen.

It's owned by a Japanese company, run by a Taiwanese shipping line called Evergreen, managed by a German company, and it flies the flag of Panama. That's normal in shipping. One ship can belong to half the world.

Now the numbers. It's almost exactly four hundred metres long. That's nearly four football pitches in a row. Stand it on its end, and it's taller than the roof of the Empire State Building.

It's almost fifty-nine metres wide. And it can carry more than twenty thousand standard containers.

If you took twenty thousand of those containers and put them in one line, end to end, that line would be about a hundred and twenty kilometres long. That's more than half the length of the Suez Canal.

Its engine is as powerful as about seventy-nine thousand horses. And at the bow there are two side thrusters, small propellers that push the front sideways. Remember those. They won't be enough.

In March twenty twenty-one, Ever Given is on its way from Malaysia to Rotterdam, in the Netherlands. The fastest way from Asia to Europe goes through one narrow ditch in the desert.

The Suez Canal cuts through Egypt and joins the Red Sea to the Mediterranean. It opened in eighteen sixty-nine.

Without it, a ship from Asia to Europe has to sail all the way around Africa, past the Cape of Good Hope. That can add up to two weeks to the trip.

So a huge share of world trade goes through here. Estimates are around twelve percent of everything traded by sea.

But here's the thing. The canal is about a hundred and ninety-three kilometres long, and for big parts of it, it's only one lane. Ships can't pass each other there. So they travel in convoys, one behind the other, like cars through roadworks. One convoy goes north, then one goes south.

Every ship takes Egyptian canal pilots on board, local experts who know the waterway. And there are speed limits, because a big ship moving too fast in shallow, narrow water starts doing strange things.

Hold on to that idea. Because that's exactly what happened.

It's early on Tuesday, the twenty-third of March. Ever Given enters the canal from the south, near the city of Suez. It's part of the northbound convoy.

The weather is bad. A sandstorm is blowing across the desert. Visibility is poor, and wind gusts are reaching more than forty knots. That's over seventy kilometres an hour.

Now think about what that wind is hitting. Ever Given is stacked high with containers, from bow to stern. That's a steel wall four hundred metres long. In strong wind, a ship like that is basically a giant sail.

Just before seven forty in the morning, the ship starts to drift. The pilot orders the rudder to port, to the left. Then hard to port.

The ship doesn't come back. It's now close to the left bank, and its speed has climbed to around thirteen knots. The canal's speed limit works out to under nine.

There's discussion on the bridge. The engine is slowed down, then pushed back up to full ahead to get more water flowing over the rudder. Hard to port again.

But the bow keeps swinging to the right. Across the canal. Towards the other bank.

At seven forty-one, Ever Given drives its bow into the east bank at about twelve knots. The stern swings round and digs into the west bank.

Four hundred metres of ship, in a canal that's narrower than the ship is long. It's wedged completely across. Kilometre one hundred and fifty-one of the Suez Canal is closed.

The first pictures shocked everyone. The bow wasn't just touching the bank. It was buried deep in it.

And at the foot of that giant bow, there was one small yellow excavator, scooping away at the sand.

That photo went around the world. It became the joke of the year. Everyone suddenly felt like the guy in that excavator, facing a problem way too big for them.

But the real problem wasn't funny. Ever Given weighs around two hundred thousand tonnes when it's loaded. The front end was sitting on sand and clay. The back end was jammed into the other side. Pushing it in the wrong place could crack the hull or bend the ship.

The first tugs arrived the same day and started pulling. The ship didn't move.

On the twenty-fifth of March, the canal authority officially stopped all traffic.

Now the line behind Ever Given starts to grow. And grow.

After a few days, at least three hundred and sixty-nine ships are waiting. By the time the canal reopens, it's more than four hundred.

The shipping newspaper Lloyd's List did a rough calculation. Around five point one billion dollars of goods a day were stuck going west, and four point five billion going east. Together, about nine point six billion dollars every single day.

Some ships couldn't wait. They gave up and turned around, taking the long way round Africa, adding days of sailing and tonnes of extra fuel.

And some of those waiting ships were carrying live animals. Sheep and cattle stuck at sea, with feed and water running down every day.

Factories in Europe waited for parts. Shops waited for stock. And everyone was asking the same question. How long?

The ship's owners hired the experts: Smit Salvage, part of the Dutch company Boskalis. Salvage teams like this are the people you call when a ship is in serious trouble.

Their plan had three parts. Dig. Pull. And wait for the right tide.

First, dig. The canal authority brought in its big cutter suction dredger, the Mashhour. It cuts into the sand under water and sucks it away through a pipe, about two thousand cubic metres an hour. Smaller dredgers and excavators worked closer to the ship.

In total, about thirty thousand cubic metres of sand were removed. That's enough to fill twelve Olympic swimming pools.

Second, pull. Eleven harbour tugs worked around the ship. Then two powerful sea-going tugs arrived to help: the Dutch Alp Guard and the Italian Carlo Magno. Alp Guard alone can pull with a force of about two hundred and eighty-five tonnes.

And there was a backup plan nobody wanted. If nothing worked, they'd have to lift off containers to make the ship lighter. But that meant bringing giant cranes to a sandy canal bank, and it could have taken weeks.

Third, the tide. And here the Moon helped. At the end of March there was a full moon close to Earth, a supermoon. That gives extra-high tides, called spring tides. A little more water under the hull can make a huge difference.

Day after day, they dug, pulled and pushed. The bow thrusters ran. The tugs pulled from different angles.

Then, very early on Monday the twenty-ninth of March, at about four thirty in the morning, the stern came free.

The bow was still stuck. But the team kept going as the tide came up again. And at five past three in the afternoon, Ever Given finally floated free.

Tugboats blew their horns. People on the banks cheered. After six days, the Suez Canal was open again. Ships started moving that same evening.

So was it just the wind? Not quite. There were a few things working together.

First, the wind. A ship with that much side area gets pushed hard.

Second, something called the bank effect. When a big ship gets close to one side of a narrow channel, water squeezes between the hull and the bank and speeds up. Fast water means low pressure. So the stern gets sucked towards the bank, and the bow gets pushed away. The faster the ship goes, the stronger it gets.

That matches what happened. The ship was close to the left bank, it was going fast, and the bow swung right across the canal.

Third, the people. In twenty twenty-three, Panama, where the ship is registered, published its investigation. It said the wind and poor visibility weren't properly treated as a risk. The ship went faster than the canal's average speed limit. No tug escort was used, even though the canal rules call for one in conditions like that.

It also said the two pilots talked to each other in Arabic, and the ship's officers couldn't follow what was being decided. And it reminded everyone of one rule: in the Suez Canal, the captain is always in command. The pilot only advises.

The canal authority and the ship's insurers argued for months about who was to blame. And that argument turned into the next part of the story.

The ship was free. But it wasn't going anywhere.

It was taken to the Great Bitter Lake, a wide part of the canal. And on the thirteenth of April, an Egyptian court ordered it seized.

The canal authority asked for more than nine hundred and sixteen million dollars. That was for the salvage work, the lost canal fees, and damage to the canal's reputation.

The owners said that was far too much. Later, the demand came down to five hundred and fifty million. The negotiations went on for weeks.

And while the lawyers talked, the twenty-five crew stayed on board, stuck in the middle of the desert with the ship. And so did all the cargo. Thousands of containers of other people's goods that just couldn't leave.

Finally, on the seventh of July, a deal was signed. The exact amount was never made public. Part of the deal was a new tugboat for the canal.

Ever Given left Egypt, and on the twenty-ninth of July it finally reached Rotterdam. More than four months after it was meant to get there.

After the blockage, Egypt sped up work to fix that narrow southern part of the canal, right where Ever Given got stuck.

The canal was widened by about forty metres along the east bank, and made deeper, over about thirty kilometres. And a new ten-kilometre second lane was dug, so more ships can pass each other.

The two-way part of the canal grew from seventy-two to eighty-two kilometres. The new lane was tested in late twenty twenty-four.

And Ever Given? It was repaired, and by the end of twenty twenty-one, it was back at work. It's still sailing today, still carrying twenty thousand containers between Asia and Europe. Through the Suez Canal.

One ship, one gust of wind, one bend in a ditch in the desert. And for six days, it showed the whole world just how much depends on a shortcut most people had never thought about.
