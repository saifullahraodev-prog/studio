# Sources: video 1 opening ("Why Half the World Doesn't Use Forks")

Checked 2026-11-15. Every spoken claim is listed with how strong it is and the exact wording used.

| # | Spoken claim | Support | Strength / wording choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "more than a billion people are eating with chopsticks" | Q. Edward Wang, *Chopsticks: A Cultural and Culinary History* (Cambridge UP, 2015): the chopsticks sphere is about 30% of the world, over 1.5 billion people. [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/chopsticks/873E0D7F010A17593EE002B2E86CD719) | Solid. The popular "1/3, 1/3, 1/3" split is NOT used; it is loosely sourced ([Straight Dope](https://boards.straightdope.com/t/cutlery-chopsticks-hands-1-3-1-3-1-3-is-this-true/119242)). |
| 2 | "Hundreds of millions are eating with their hands" | Estimates vary; "substantially higher than 250 million" ([DelightedCooking](https://delightedcooking.com/does-one-third-of-the-worlds-population-eat-with-their-hands.htm)) | Deliberately conservative ("hundreds of millions"). **Upgrade to a scholarly source before publishing.** |
| 3 | "the fork? It's the newcomer" | The fork spread beyond European elites only in the late 17th–18th centuries ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/dinner-fork-history)), versus chopsticks at 3,000+ years | Solid as a comparison. |
| 4 | Venice, 11th century, Byzantine bride, golden fork at her wedding feast | National Geographic (Maria Argyropoula, married the Doge's son, two-pronged golden fork); also [The Takeout](https://www.thetakeout.com/1762961/europe-fork-history/) | The year (often "1004") and her exact identity are disputed, so the script says only "the eleventh century" and "a Byzantine bride". |
| 5 | A churchman: God gave us natural forks, our fingers | National Geographic quotes a Venetian clergyman's sermon: "God in his wisdom has provided man with natural forks—his fingers." Often attributed to St Peter Damian (via J. J. Norwich, *A History of Venice*) | The script says "a churchman" because the name is contested. The card quote is shortened. |
| 6 | "Europe agreed with him, for centuries" / "five hundred years later, most Europeans still ate with a knife and their fingers" | Italy's elite adopted forks during the Renaissance; France early 17th century; beyond elites late 17th–18th century; standard by the 19th ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/dinner-fork-history)) | Solid. "Most Europeans" in c.1500 is accurate, since outside Italian elites forks were rare. |
| 7 | 1608, Thomas Coryat saw Italians eating with forks and brought one home; nickname "Furcifer" (Latin: fork-bearer; also rascal) | *Coryat's Crudities* (1611) on his 1608 travels; "Furcifer" nickname ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Coryat), [Shakespeare Birthplace Trust](https://shakespeare.org.uk/explore-shakespeare/blogs/coryats-crudities-tourism-1611-style)) | Solid for the anecdote. The script does NOT say "first fork in England" (contested, per [Folger](https://www.folger.edu/blogs/shakespeare-and-beyond/thomas-coryate-mughal-emperor-court-backpacker/)). Furcifer = "fork-bearer" / "rascal" (classical Latin "gallows-bird, rascal"). |
| 8 | "The oldest [chopsticks] ever found are over three thousand years old" | Six bronze chopsticks from Yinxu (Anyang), Shang period, c. 1200 BC, thought to be cooking tools ([Wikipedia: Chopsticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chopsticks)) | Solid (about 3,200 years). |
| 9 | "the cutting happened in the kitchen, not at the table" | Chinese cooking cut food to bite size before serving, so no knives were needed at the table (general culinary history; Wang 2015) | **Add a page reference from Wang before publishing.** |
| 10 | "One popular theory: small pieces cook faster and save fuel" | [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/legacy_blog/the-history-of-chopsticks/) (via the California Academy of Sciences): fuel scarcity around 400 BC led cooks to cut food small | Framed explicitly as **"one popular theory"**. It is not settled history. |

**Not claims:** the map's country colours are simplified placeholder data. The period rooms and costumes are placeholders. The 11th-century wedding is shown as an exhibit (the golden fork), not as a staged scene.
