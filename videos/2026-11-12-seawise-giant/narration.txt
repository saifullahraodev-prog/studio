THE SHIP GUY - The Biggest Ship Ever Built Was Bombed, Then Came Back
Narration only. Read it naturally; leave a 1-2 second pause between sections (the [headings] are not read).

[Hook]

In May 1988, fighter jets fired missiles at the biggest ship humans have ever built.

The ship burst into flames, and its owners wrote it off as a total loss.

This ship was four hundred and fifty-eight metres long. Stand it on its end, and it's taller than the Petronas Towers.

It was so big it couldn't fit through the Suez Canal, the Panama Canal, or even the English Channel when it was fully loaded.

If the captain went full astern at top speed, it needed about nine kilometres to stop.

And that fire wasn't the end. This ship was rebuilt, and sailed again under four new names.

So how do you build something this big... and why did the world decide never to build one again?


[The ship nobody wanted]

It's 1974, in Japan. At the Oppama shipyard near Tokyo, Sumitomo Heavy Industries is building a monster.

It doesn't even have a name yet. Just a number: hull 1016.

It's a supertanker made for one job: carrying as much crude oil as possible in one trip. Bigger ship, more oil, cheaper oil per barrel. That was the idea.

But during sea trials, something goes wrong. When the ship goes astern, in reverse, it shakes violently.

The Greek shipowner who ordered it refuses to take it. Lawyers get involved. And for years, one of the largest objects ever built just... sits there.

Eventually, the shipyard sells it under a new name: Oppama. And that's when a shipping tycoon from Hong Kong walks in with a wild idea.


[Make it even bigger]

His name is C. Y. Tung, the founder of the OOCL shipping line. He looks at one of the biggest ships in the world, and he says: make it bigger.

So the shipyard does something that sounds impossible. They lengthen the ship. They cut into the hull and build in a brand new section of steel.

That one change adds about a hundred and forty-six thousand tonnes of cargo capacity. That's more than many normal tankers can carry in total.

In 1979, it returns to the water with a new name: Seawise Giant.


[Just how big?]

Let's put this in numbers you can actually picture.

Length: four hundred and fifty-eight metres. That's longer than four and a half football fields laid end to end.

Width: about sixty-nine metres. You could park a twenty-lane highway across its deck.

Under the water, when fully loaded, the hull went down almost twenty-five metres. That's like sinking an eight-storey building below the waves.

It could carry about four point one million barrels of oil. Its deck covered more than thirty-one thousand square metres, around four football pitches of steel.

Its rudder alone weighed two hundred and thirty tonnes. The propeller weighed fifty tonnes.

And when it was fully loaded, the whole ship weighed more than six hundred and fifty thousand tonnes.

A steam turbine of fifty thousand horsepower pushed all of that through the sea at about sixteen and a half knots, around thirty kilometres an hour.

That sounds slow. Until you try to stop.


[The problem with being too big]

One of its captains said that from full speed, it needed about nine kilometres to come to a stop.

To turn around, it needed a circle about three kilometres wide.

And it couldn't go where most ships go. Too wide for the Panama Canal. Too deep for the Suez Canal. Fully loaded, even the English Channel was too shallow.

So the biggest ship in the world was locked out of some of the most important shipping routes in the world.

But in the 1980s, there was one place where a giant floating oil tank was exactly what someone needed. And that place was a war zone.


[The war]

Iran and Iraq are at war. Both sides start attacking oil tankers in the Persian Gulf, trying to choke off each other's money. It becomes known as the Tanker War.

Seawise Giant is used to store and move Iranian crude oil. On the fourteenth of May, 1988, it's anchored off Larak Island, near the Strait of Hormuz.

Iraqi aircraft arrive. They fire French-made Exocet missiles.

The missiles hit. Fire spreads across the oil on the water. The biggest ship in the world becomes a burning wreck.

The insurers declare it a total loss. For almost any other ship, that would be the end of the story.


[The comeback]

After the war ends, a Norwegian company looks at the burned giant and buys it anyway.

It's towed to Singapore, where the Keppel shipyard repairs it. In October 1991, it goes back to work with a new name: Happy Giant.

The same year, Norwegian shipowner Jørgen Jahre buys it for thirty-nine million dollars and renames it Jahre Viking. For more than ten years, it carries oil around the world again.

In 2004, it's sold once more and renamed Knock Nevis. This time it doesn't sail. It's anchored off Qatar and used as a floating oil storage tank.


[Why we stopped building giants]

So if one giant ship can carry so much oil, why don't we build them like this anymore?

In the late sixties and early seventies, shipping companies ordered dozens of these ultra-large tankers. Then the 1973 oil crisis hit, and the oil trade they were built for changed.

Big ships only make money when they're full and moving. A ship this size could only use a few ports, couldn't take shortcuts, and took a long time to fill.

France built four giants of the Batillus class, each over five hundred and fifty thousand tonnes deadweight. Three of them were scrapped after less than ten years carrying oil.

Today's big oil tankers are smaller. They give up some size to get more flexibility. It turned out that being able to go almost anywhere is worth more than being the biggest.


[The end]

In 2009, the old giant makes one last trip, under its final name: Mont. On the twenty-second of December, it's driven onto the beach at Alang, in India. A year later, it's gone, cut apart piece by piece.

But one piece survived. Its thirty-six-tonne anchor is on display in Hong Kong today.

In 2017, a floating gas plant called Prelude finally beat its length. But Prelude is fixed to the seabed. It was built to sit in one place, not to sail the world.

So it was too big for the canals, too big for most ports, and the world decided it was too big to build again. And it was still the longest ship that ever crossed the world's oceans.
