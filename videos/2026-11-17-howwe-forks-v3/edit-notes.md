## 1. Factual corrections
1. **Oldest chopsticks.** v3: "the oldest chopsticks ever found are bronze, over 3,000 years old." Wrong. Wang (Cambridge UP, 2015) cites bone sticks, possibly chopsticks, about 7,000 years old. Anyang is the oldest *bronze* pair.
2. **Why chopsticks took over.** v3: Han dynasty, rice. Scholarly view (Wang): the spoon was China's main utensil for millennia. Chopsticks became the main tool about 1,000 years ago, mainly because wheat noodles and dumplings spread, with rice also growing.
3. **Venice fork.** v3: "A churchman was horrified. God, he said, had already given us forks. Our fingers." That quote isn't in the primary source. The real source is Peter Damian's letter, written decades later, using her as a warning against luxury. Quote removed. Her servants cutting her food is added; it's in the source.
4. **Louis XIV.** v3 implied a kingdom-wide ban. Now "in part of his kingdom" (the scope is disputed; Wikipedia says the Flemish provinces).
5. **Four prongs "in Germany".** Only one weak source. Removed; now "by the late 1700s" (Cooper Hewitt).
6. **Silver chopsticks.** v3: silver reacts "not to arsenic itself", so the legend is false. More precise: some old arsenic poisons contained sulphur, so the legend is half right, and silver was also about status.
7. **French court "early 1600s".** Date dropped (sources vary); now "crept north, into France".
8. **Brace.** Now cited properly (*American Anthropologist*, 1986). The counter-view (softer food since early farming, *Science* 2019) is spoken in one line.
9. **Kept, with clear labels:** Richelieu = "tradition says"; fuel = "one popular explanation"; pasta = "one popular explanation"; silver = "legend says"; Brace = "his explanation… still debated".
