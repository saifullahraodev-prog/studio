It's the sixteenth of February, twenty twenty-two. A ship is crossing the Atlantic Ocean with almost four thousand brand-new cars on board. Porsches. Bentleys. Even a few Lamborghinis.

At eight thirty in the morning, a smoke alarm goes off.

Two weeks later, the ship and every single car on it are at the bottom of the ocean.

Nobody died. But the cargo was worth hundreds of millions of dollars, and four years later, a court still couldn't say for sure what started the fire.

So what happened on that ship? To find out, we need to go back to the morning of the alarm.

The ship was called the Felicity Ace. It was a car carrier, about two hundred metres long, and from the outside it looks like a giant floating shoebox.

Inside, it's basically a multi-storey car park. Eleven decks of cars, stacked on top of each other, with room for more than five thousand.

The cars are driven on board up a big ramp at the back, parked just centimetres apart, and strapped to the deck so they can't slide around in a storm.

On this trip, it had loaded three thousand nine hundred and sixty-five vehicles in Emden, in Germany, bound for Rhode Island, in the United States. About three and a half thousand nautical miles.

Most were from the Volkswagen Group. Around eleven hundred were Porsches. A hundred and eighty-nine were Bentleys. And fifteen were the Lamborghini Aventador Ultimae, the very last version of a famous V12 supercar.

One insurance analyst estimated the cars alone were worth around four hundred million dollars.

And there was one more detail. Five hundred and seventy-four of those cars were electric.

Keep that number in mind.

The trip started rough. Three days out, the ship hit a gale, with waves around three metres high. The captain changed course to stop the ship slamming into them.

Then the weather calmed down.

The crew did their safety rounds through the car decks, day and night. One in the morning. Five in the morning. Seven in the morning. Nothing unusual.

At ten to eight, the third officer came up to the bridge to take over the watch. The captain was there too. Everything was normal.

And then, at eight thirty, the alarm.

The smoke detector said: deck one, at the back of the ship.

And on this ship, deck one isn't the bottom. The car decks are counted from the top down, so deck one is the highest car deck, more than thirty metres above the bottom of the hull.

From the bridge, nobody could see any smoke. So the officer radioed a sailor who was already doing a safety round and asked him to go and check.

The sailor went down the stairs and opened the door to deck one.

Black smoke. And between the rows of cars, flames. Three or four cars already burning. And small explosions.

The sailor grabbed a fire extinguisher, but couldn't get anywhere near the flames. The smoke was too thick. So the door went shut again, and the alarm went out to the whole ship.

The fire team got ready fast, with breathing sets and fire suits. They opened the door again. They couldn't see a thing.

The crew started the emergency fire pump and sprayed water on the outside of the deck above, to cool it down. Then the captain went to plan B. The ship's fixed firefighting system.

Car carriers don't use sprinklers like a building. This one used high-expansion foam.

The idea is simple. A pump mixes foam liquid with water, and big electric fans blow air through it. That air puffs the mixture up to hundreds of times its size, into a huge wave of foam that fills the whole deck and smothers the fire, like the world's biggest bubble bath.

The first engineer ran to the room where the foam system was, and started the pumps. Foam liquid was flowing. The level in the tank was going down.

But the fans on deck one didn't start.

No fans, no foam. Just liquid running out of the pipes.

The engineer ran up to the electrical room to check the fan circuits and breakers. Still nothing. The fans would not start.

Meanwhile, the fire team was reporting that the fire was out of control. So the captain made the call.

Abandon ship.

The ship sent out a distress call. A tanker called the Resilient Warrior, on her way to Rotterdam, answered and turned around.

Within about an hour, all twenty-two crew had climbed down a rope ladder to safety.

Later, a Portuguese Air Force helicopter flew them to the island of Faial, in the Azores. Nobody was hurt.

The Felicity Ace was left on her own, burning, in the middle of the Atlantic.

So why was this fire so hard to stop?

First, a car deck is one giant open space. There are no walls between the cars to stop a fire spreading, and air can flow along the whole deck.

Second, every car is full of things that burn. Fuel, plastic, foam seats, tyres. Thousands of cars means thousands of fires waiting to happen.

And third, the electric cars.

A lithium-ion battery that overheats can go into what's called thermal runaway. One cell heats up, sets off the next one, and the next one, and the battery keeps feeding its own fire. It can even reignite after it looks like it's out.

The investigators said it plainly. Car carriers, at the time, were not prepared to put out fires on electric and hybrid cars. There was no special equipment, and no special procedure.

But here's the thing. Car carrier fires didn't start with electric cars.

Two years earlier, in June twenty twenty, another car carrier, the Höegh Xiamen, caught fire in the port of Jacksonville, in Florida. She was full of used cars, most of them running on petrol.

America's National Transportation Safety Board found the cause. The batteries in some of those used cars were supposed to be disconnected and secured before loading. One wasn't done properly, and an electrical fault started the fire.

That ship burned for more than a week. Nine firefighters were injured, and the ship and all two thousand four hundred and twenty cars were a total loss.

So any car carrier fire is hard. Electric cars just add one more problem nobody had fully solved.

The owners called in a salvage company, Smit Salvage, from the Netherlands. They flew a team of sixteen experts to the Azores.

Tugs reached the ship and started spraying her with water, to cool the hull from the outside.

Day after day, the fire kept burning inside. Little by little, the flames died down.

When it was safe enough, a salvage team climbed on board from a small patrol boat to see what was left.

Then a big salvage tug called Bear got a line on board and started towing her toward calmer water off the Azores.

Then, on the first of March, she started to lean to one side.

At eleven minutes past nine in the morning, the Felicity Ace sank. And all three thousand nine hundred and sixty-five cars went with her.

The tugs stayed for a few days, watching for oil leaking from the wreck. The operator said no new oil spills were seen, and after that the site was watched by satellite and from the air.

So who paid?

Some of the people waiting for a new Porsche or Bentley had to wait a lot longer.

And Lamborghini did something unusual. It had already finished building the Aventador. But fifteen of the last ones were now on the sea floor. So it restarted production, just to build those fifteen cars again.

But the biggest question was still open. Who was to blame?

The ship flew the flag of Panama, so Panama's maritime authority investigated.

According to the crew, the fire started on an electric Porsche Taycan.

But the report also said the reason the car caught fire was unknown. It listed possible causes, like a faulty battery cell, damage during assembly, or a short circuit, but it couldn't prove any of them.

The report also asked a hard question. Could the fire have been stopped early? A crew member had taken a photo inside deck one, and in it, the investigators could still see the cars and the lights. They thought a fire team might have been able to go in, use the hoses already fitted on the deck, and stop the fire spreading from car to car. But they also found that the crew had no special training for car deck fires, or for electric cars, and little confidence going into a space like that.

And the fans that never started? The investigators thought their motor sat on deck one, near the fire, and its cables were probably damaged by the heat.

The ship's owner and insurers took Porsche to court in Germany, asking for about thirty million euros. Their argument: Porsche should have warned them about the risks of shipping electric cars, and how to carry them safely.

In May twenty twenty-six, a court in Stuttgart rejected the claim. The judges said they were not convinced that a Taycan had started the fire. That ruling can still be appealed, and a second case in another German court is still going.

So, four years later, nobody has officially proven exactly what started it.

And this wasn't the last time.

In July twenty twenty-three, another car carrier, the Fremantle Highway, caught fire off the coast of the Netherlands. One crew member died.

Since then, Europe's maritime safety agency has published guidance on carrying electric cars on ships, and the International Maritime Organization has been working on new fire rules for car carriers.

Better detection. Better ways to spot a hot car early. Better ways to fight a fire before it spreads.
