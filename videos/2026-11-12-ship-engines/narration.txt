Remember the tugboats from the harbour? They're small, but they have one job: push and pull ships that weigh a hundred times more than they do. A modern harbour tug like PSA Thor in Singapore has two diesel engines, each making about two and a half thousand kilowatts. Together, that's nearly seven thousand horsepower in a boat you could park in a car park. The clever part is underneath. Its propellers sit in drives that can spin all the way around, so the tug can push in any direction, even sideways. Its pulling force, called bollard pull, is about eighty tonnes. That's like a rope tied to forty cars being dragged at once.

Next, the engines that run ferries, offshore ships and the power systems of big vessels: medium-speed four-stroke diesels. They work like the engine in a truck, just much bigger, with each cylinder as big as a washing machine. In 2015, one of them, the Wärtsilä 31, set a Guinness World Record as the most fuel-efficient four-stroke diesel engine in the world. Depending on the model, one engine makes between about four and ten megawatts. That's enough to power thousands of homes.

Now things get fast. Many warships don't use a normal ship engine at all. They use a jet engine. The General Electric LM2500 was developed from the CF6, an engine that powers big airliners. Instead of pushing a plane, it spins a propeller shaft. One LM2500 makes about thirty-three thousand horsepower, and it weighs less than five tonnes. That's a huge amount of power for its size. An American Arleigh Burke-class destroyer carries four of them. And by 2004, more than thirty navies had used over a thousand of these turbines. The downside? Jet engines drink fuel. They're perfect for speed, terrible for long, cheap trips. That's why you'll almost never find one in a cargo ship, where every litre of fuel is money.

The world's biggest cruise ship, Icon of the Seas, doesn't have one big engine. It has six. They burn liquefied natural gas or diesel, and together they make about sixty-seven megawatts, roughly ninety thousand horsepower. But none of them is connected to a propeller. They make electricity, like a power station. That electricity runs the lights, the pools, the restaurants, and giant electric motors in pods under the ship that turn the propellers. So the biggest cruise ship in the world is really a floating power station with a hotel on top.

Remember the level that doesn't burn fuel? This is it. A Nimitz-class aircraft carrier is powered by two nuclear reactors. Each one makes steam that drives turbines, and together they send around two hundred and eighty thousand horsepower to four giant propellers. And they don't need to refuel every few weeks. The reactors are built to run for about twenty to twenty-five years before they get new fuel. That's the most powerful ship propulsion on this list. But it's not one engine. It's two reactors and a whole plant of turbines. Which brings us to the final level: the biggest single engine ever put inside a ship.

This is the Wärtsilä-Sulzer RT-flex96C. In its fourteen-cylinder version, it's the largest diesel engine of its kind ever built. Slow, huge, and simple: that's why engines like this push most of the world's cargo.
