## 0. Hook (0:00–0:35)

This piston weighs five and a half tonnes.
*[a piston taller than a person drops into frame; counter "5.5 t"]*

The crankshaft it turns weighs three hundred tonnes. And the engine it belongs to is taller than a four-storey building.
*[engine silhouette rises next to a 4-storey building]*

But here's the strange part. At full power, it turns only about a hundred times a minute. Slower than your car engine when it's just sitting at a red light.
*[slow rotating crank vs a car rev counter]*

Today we're climbing every level of ship engine, from the little motor on a fishing boat, all the way up to this monster.
*[ladder graphic, levels 1–7, top level hidden]*

And one of these levels is so powerful, it doesn't burn any fuel at all.

## 1. Level 1 – The outboard motor (0:35–1:20)

Let's start small. This is an outboard motor: an engine, a gearbox and a propeller in one box, clamped to the back of a boat.
*[small boat, outboard on the stern, you steering]*

The smallest ones make just a few horsepower. You can carry them with one hand.

But outboards have grown up. Mercury's V12 Verado makes six hundred horsepower from a single motor. It weighs about as much as a small car, and costs more than many of them.
*[GFX: "600 hp" counter, size bar outboard vs car]*

Fast boats hang two, three, even four of these on the back. Four of them is two thousand four hundred horsepower on one small boat.
*[boat stern with four outboards; counter "2,400 hp"]*

But even six hundred horsepower is tiny compared to what you need to push something heavy.

## 2. Level 2 – The tugboat (1:20–2:10)

Remember the tugboats from the harbour? They're small, but they have one job: push and pull ships that weigh a hundred times more than they do.
*[tug pushing a giant tanker's bow]*

A modern harbour tug like PSA Thor in Singapore has two diesel engines, each making about two and a half thousand kilowatts. Together, that's nearly seven thousand horsepower in a boat you could park in a car park.
*[GFX: two engines, counter "≈ 6,800 hp"]*

The clever part is underneath. Its propellers sit in drives that can spin all the way around, so the tug can push in any direction, even sideways.
*[top-down: tug with 360° arrows]*

Its pulling force, called bollard pull, is about eighty tonnes. That's like a rope tied to forty cars being dragged at once.
*[GFX: rope to a row of cars]*

## 3. Level 3 – The workhorse four-stroke (2:10–2:55)

Next, the engines that run ferries, offshore ships and the power systems of big vessels: medium-speed four-stroke diesels.
*[ferry at sea, cut-away showing a row of cylinders]*

They work like the engine in a truck, just much bigger, with each cylinder as big as a washing machine.

In 2015, one of them, the Wärtsilä 31, set a Guinness World Record as the most fuel-efficient four-stroke diesel engine in the world.
*[GFX: record badge "GUINNESS WORLD RECORD 2015"]*

Depending on the model, one engine makes between about four and ten megawatts. That's enough to power thousands of homes.
*[GFX: "4–10 MW"]*

## 4. Level 4 – The jet engine at sea (2:55–3:45)

Now things get fast. Many warships don't use a normal ship engine at all. They use a jet engine.
*[destroyer cutting through waves at speed]*

The General Electric LM2500 was developed from the CF6, an engine that powers big airliners. Instead of pushing a plane, it spins a propeller shaft.
*[GFX: airliner engine morphs into a ship turbine]*

One LM2500 makes about thirty-three thousand horsepower, and it weighs less than five tonnes. That's a huge amount of power for its size.
*[GFX: "33,600 hp" vs "4.7 t"]*

An American Arleigh Burke-class destroyer carries four of them. And by 2004, more than thirty navies had used over a thousand of these turbines.
*[four turbines lighting up in a destroyer cut-away]*

The downside? Jet engines drink fuel. They're perfect for speed, terrible for long, cheap trips. That's why you'll almost never find one in a cargo ship, where every litre of fuel is money.

## 5. Level 5 – The floating power station (3:45–4:30)

The world's biggest cruise ship, Icon of the Seas, doesn't have one big engine. It has six.
*[Icon of the Seas side view, six engines glowing below]*

They burn liquefied natural gas or diesel, and together they make about sixty-seven megawatts, roughly ninety thousand horsepower.
*[GFX: "≈ 67 MW"]*

But none of them is connected to a propeller. They make electricity, like a power station. That electricity runs the lights, the pools, the restaurants, and giant electric motors in pods under the ship that turn the propellers.
*[cut-away: engines → cables → pods under the hull]*

So the biggest cruise ship in the world is really a floating power station with a hotel on top.

## 6. Level 6 – The engine that doesn't burn anything (4:30–5:20)

Remember the level that doesn't burn fuel? This is it.
*[aircraft carrier at sea, jets on deck]*

A Nimitz-class aircraft carrier is powered by two nuclear reactors. Each one makes steam that drives turbines, and together they send around two hundred and eighty thousand horsepower to four giant propellers.
*[cut-away: 2 reactors → steam → 4 shafts; counter "≈ 280,000 hp"]*

And they don't need to refuel every few weeks. The reactors are built to run for about twenty to twenty-five years before they get new fuel.
*[GFX: calendar flipping years]*

That's the most powerful ship propulsion on this list. But it's not one engine. It's two reactors and a whole plant of turbines.
*[you pointing at the plant: "NOT ONE ENGINE"]*

Which brings us to the final level: the biggest single engine ever put inside a ship.

## 7. Final level – The 2,300-tonne monster (5:20–6:40)

This is the Wärtsilä-Sulzer RT-flex96C. In its fourteen-cylinder version, it's the largest diesel engine of its kind ever built.
*[the engine revealed, you tiny at its base]*

It's thirteen and a half metres tall, almost twenty-seven metres long, and it weighs more than two thousand three hundred tonnes.
*[GFX: engine vs four-storey building, vs a tennis court for length]*

Each cylinder is ninety-six centimetres wide. Each piston moves two and a half metres up and down. You could stand inside one of the cylinders.
*[cut-away: you standing inside a cylinder]*

The piston from the start of this video? Together with its rod, it's six metres tall. And the crankshaft, the part that turns all that up-and-down motion into spinning, weighs three hundred tonnes. That's as heavy as about two hundred cars.
*[GFX: crankshaft on a scale vs a pile of 200 cars]*

Together, the fourteen cylinders make about one hundred and seven thousand horsepower, from one engine.
*[GFX: counter "107,390 hp"]*

This engine was built for the Emma Maersk, a container ship almost four hundred metres long. At full power, it burns about fourteen thousand litres of heavy fuel oil every hour.
*[Emma Maersk at sea; fuel gauge draining]*

And remember how slowly it turns? About a hundred revolutions a minute. That's not a weakness. It's the whole point. A giant propeller works best when it turns slowly, so this engine is connected straight to the propeller shaft, with no gearbox at all. Slow, huge, and simple: that's why engines like this push most of the world's cargo.
*[slow crank turning a huge propeller directly]*

## 8. The end (6:40–7:05)

So from a six-hundred-horsepower outboard to a two-thousand-three-hundred-tonne engine, every level of ship has the engine that fits its job: speed, pulling power, electricity, endurance, or pure size.
*[the full ladder lit up]*

Here's one last comparison. Seawise Giant, the longest ship ever built, had a steam turbine of about fifty thousand horsepower. This one engine makes more than twice that.
*[Seawise Giant next to Emma Maersk, "50,000 hp" vs "107,390 hp"]*

If you want to see what happens when an engine like this suddenly stops in the middle of the ocean, watch this video next.
*[end screen: engine-failure video + subscribe]*

## Fact notes (please read before recording)

1. **"Largest diesel engine of its kind"**: the 14-cylinder RT-flex96C is usually called the world's largest / most
   powerful reciprocating (piston) engine. A few newer engines (e.g. 12-cylinder MAN/Wärtsilä X92 types) come close,
   so the script says "of its kind" and "biggest single engine ever put inside a ship" rather than "most powerful engine".
2. **Engine speed**: sources give 102 rpm at full power (some say up to 120). Script says "about a hundred".
3. **Outboards**: Mercury's 600 hp V12 is Mercury's biggest. A discontinued Seven Marine outboard made 627 hp, so the
   script does NOT call the Verado "the most powerful outboard ever".
4. **Tug power**: "nearly seven thousand horsepower" = 2 × 2,525 kW ≈ 6,770 hp. "Forty cars" assumes ~2 t per car
   pulled with 80 t: a picture, not a measurement.
5. **Icon of the Seas**: sources disagree on the per-engine breakdown; the combined ~67.5 MW figure is the most
   consistent, so only the total is used.
6. **Nimitz power**: 280,000 shp = 2 × 140,000 (my sum of the per-reactor figure). Refuelling: sources say 20–25 years,
   so the script says "about twenty to twenty-five years".
7. **Seawise Giant comparison**: 50,000 hp steam turbine (from our last video's sources) vs 107,390 hp.
8. **"Taller than a four-storey building"**: 13.5 m vs ~3 m per storey.
9. **Car at a red light**: car engines idle at roughly 600–900 rpm, well above ~100 rpm.
10. **"About two hundred cars"**: 300 t crankshaft ÷ ~1.5 t per car. Piston "six metres tall with its rod" = the 6 m
    piston height in the RTA96-C article.
11. **"Engines like this push most of the world's cargo"**: large low-speed two-stroke diesels directly coupled to the
    propeller are the standard propulsion for big cargo ships (tankers, bulk carriers, container ships). General
    industry fact, stated without a number.
