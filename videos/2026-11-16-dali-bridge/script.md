## 0. Hook (0:00–0:30)

At twenty-five past one in the morning, a ship as long as three football pitches went completely dark.
*[the Dali at night under the lit bridge; every light on the ship blinks out]*

Four minutes later, a bridge that had stood for forty-seven years was lying in the river.
*[the bridge silhouette, then the gap where it was]*

Six men working on that bridge never went home.
*[a quiet shot: a road-work sign with a hard hat beside it]*

And when investigators finally found what started it all, it was a piece of plastic smaller than your finger.
*[the Ship Guy holds up something tiny between two fingers, squints at it]*

So how does a tiny label take down a giant bridge? To understand that, we need to go back to that night, and on board that ship.

## 1. The ship and the night (0:30–1:41)

The ship is called Dali. It's a container ship, three hundred metres long, built in South Korea in twenty fifteen.
*[side view of the Dali sailing, the Ship Guy on the dock waving it off]*

On the night of the twenty-sixth of March, twenty twenty-four, it's leaving the Port of Baltimore, in the United States. It's carrying about four thousand seven hundred containers, and it's heading for Sri Lanka.
*[map: Baltimore → across the oceans → Sri Lanka]*

On board are twenty-one crew, and two local harbour pilots, experts who guide big ships through the harbour.
*[bridge interior: crew, two pilots, the Ship Guy with his coffee]*

In a big port, ships don't find their own way in and out. Every harbour has its own bends, shallow spots and bridges, so local pilots come on board and give the steering orders until the ship reaches open water.
*[the pilots pointing at a chart of the river; the Ship Guy nodding, taking notes]*

Ahead of it is the Francis Scott Key Bridge. It's about two point six kilometres long, and it carries a busy highway across the river.
*[the bridge from the side, lit up, cars moving]*

And it has a famous name. In eighteen fourteen, Francis Scott Key watched a battle at Fort McHenry, just up this same river, and wrote the poem that became America's national anthem. Every summer, a red, white and blue buoy is placed in the water near the spot where he watched.
*[the old fort with a giant flag; a striped buoy bobbing near the bridge; the Ship Guy saluting]*

And on the bridge that night, a small road crew is doing a normal night-shift job: filling potholes.
*[workers in orange vests by a work truck, cones]*

Everything is normal. Until it isn't.

## 2. Lights out (1:41–3:06)

At twenty-five past one, when the ship is only about three ship lengths away from the bridge, every light on the Dali goes out.
*[bridge interior: screens die, lights off, everyone frozen. A distance line: 3 ship lengths to the bridge]*

To understand why that's so dangerous, you need to know one thing about big ships. A modern ship is basically a floating power station.
*[the Ship Guy at a whiteboard: ship cut-away with generators glowing]*

Generators make the electricity, and that electricity runs almost everything. The steering. The lights. The radios. And the pumps that keep the main engine alive.
*[cut-away: wires light up from the generators to the rudder, the radio, the pumps]*

The main engine is cooled by water, and that water is pushed around by electric pumps. So when the power dies, the cooling stops, and the main engine shuts itself down.
*[the engine's gauges drop; the propeller slows and stops]*

No engine means no propeller pushing water past the rudder. And without that flow, the rudder can barely steer.
*[top-down: water arrows past the rudder fade away; the Ship Guy turns a big wheel that does nothing]*

So why not just stop? Because a ship this size can't stop like a car. There are no brakes. When it's fully loaded, the Dali and its cargo weigh well over a hundred thousand tonnes, and all of that keeps gliding forward.
*[the Ship Guy stamps on an imaginary brake pedal on the bridge; nothing happens. The ship keeps sliding]*

Even at a walking pace, that much weight doesn't want to stop. Imagine trying to stop a sliding block of flats with your hands.
*[the Ship Guy on the quay, pushing against the moving hull; his feet slide along the ground]*

The pilots try everything they can. They order the rudder hard over to turn away, and they drop an anchor to drag the ship and slow it down.
*[the anchor splashes down; the chain runs out; the ship barely slows]*

But an anchor isn't a parking brake. It digs in and drags, and that takes time. Three ship lengths is not much time.
*[the anchor ploughing a groove along the riverbed; the bridge getting closer]*

The crew get some power back within about a minute. And then, it goes out again.
*[lights flicker on… and off. The Ship Guy's hat flies off]*

## 3. Ninety seconds (3:06–4:01)

At twenty-seven minutes past one, a mayday goes out on the radio. The ship has lost power, and it's heading for the bridge.
*[radio waves from the ship; calm, serious music]*

What happened next probably saved a lot of lives. The message reached the police, and officers at both ends of the bridge stopped the traffic.
*[police cars at both ends, lights on, cars stopping]*

They had about ninety seconds. And in that time, no more cars drove onto the bridge.
*[a timer: 90 → 0]*

One officer even radioed that he would drive onto the bridge to warn the road crew. There wasn't enough time.
*[a police car at the end of the bridge, its lights on the empty road ahead]*

Later, the NTSB said the quick actions of the pilots, the dispatchers and the police prevented a much bigger loss of life.
*[the radio room, the dispatcher's microphone]*

But the road crew in the middle didn't get the warning in time.
*[the work truck in the middle of the bridge, workers unaware]*

At about one twenty-nine, the Dali, moving at around seven knots, a fast walking pace, hits one of the bridge's main supports.
*[top-down: the bow meets the pier. A short, quiet impact sound]*

The bridge comes down in about thirty seconds.
*[side view, slow: the truss sags and falls into the river; then stillness]*

Two people were rescued from the water and the wreckage. Six road workers died.
*[six small hard hats in a row; no music for a moment]*

## 4. The search (4:01–4:31)

Divers went into the river that same night. The water was dark and cold, and full of twisted steel and broken concrete.
*[underwater: dive lights in murky water, steel beams at odd angles]*

The search was slow and dangerous. Some of the wreckage was so tangled that divers couldn't reach it until salvage crews had cut it apart.
*[a diver stopping at a wall of twisted metal]*

Two of the workers were found the next day, inside their truck at the bottom of the river. The others were found over the following weeks.
*[a calendar turning pages, quietly]*

The last of the six was recovered on the seventh of May. Six weeks after the bridge fell.
*[the river at dawn, calm; a single flower on the water]*

## 5. Why a whole bridge fell (4:31–5:32)

So how can one hit bring down a bridge that's more than two kilometres long?
*[the Ship Guy, serious, by the bridge diagram]*

The middle part of the Key Bridge was a continuous steel truss, one long piece resting on a few supports. Take away one of those supports, and the long piece can't hold itself up. It all goes together.
*[diagram: truss on piers; one pier removed; the truss folds]*

Big bridges over shipping lanes are protected by barriers around their supports, called dolphins. They're there to catch or push away a ship before it reaches the bridge.
*[top-down: round concrete dolphins around a pier, a small ship bouncing off]*

On the newest bridges, the supports even sit on artificial islands of rock, so a ship runs aground long before it can touch the bridge.
*[a ship's bow sliding to a stop on a rock island; the Ship Guy giving a thumbs up]*

But the Key Bridge was built in the nineteen-seventies. Ships have become much bigger since then, and its protection was far too small for a ship like the Dali.
*[side-by-side: a 1970s ship next to the Dali, dolphins tiny in comparison]*

Later, the investigators calculated that the risk of this bridge collapsing after a ship strike was almost thirty times higher than what's acceptable for a bridge this important. And nobody had ever checked.
*[a risk meter swings far into red: "30×"]*

And here's the frustrating part. America had already learned this lesson once, the hard way.

## 6. It happened before (5:32–6:38)

It's the ninth of May, nineteen eighty, in Tampa Bay, Florida. Early in the morning, a sudden storm hits the bay.
*[a dark squall over the water, rain sheets, a freighter in the gloom]*

A cargo ship called the Summit Venture is heading for the Sunshine Skyway Bridge. In the storm, the crew can barely see, and the ship's radar fails.
*[bridge of the freighter: the radar screen goes to static; the window is just rain]*

The ship hits one of the bridge's supports. A long section of the bridge falls into the bay.
*[side view: the span drops; headlights on the road above stop at the edge]*

Cars and a bus drove off the broken edge in the storm. Thirty-five people died.
*[a quiet, dark shot of the broken road edge in the rain]*

One man survived. His pickup truck fell with the road, hit the side of the ship, and sank. He swam back to the surface, and the ship's own crew pulled him out of the water.
*[a small pickup silhouette falling; then a rope and a life ring thrown from the freighter's deck]*

That disaster changed the rules. After it, engineers in the United States created a way to calculate how likely a bridge is to be hit by a ship, and how strong its protection needs to be. The new Sunshine Skyway was built with huge protective barriers around its supports.
*[the new Skyway with big concrete islands around its piers; the Ship Guy nods at a rulebook]*

But those rules were made for new bridges. The Key Bridge had been built before them. And nobody went back to check it against the bigger and bigger ships coming through.
*[the Key Bridge with a sticky note "built 1977" next to the rulebook "1991"]*

## 7. The piece of plastic (6:38–8:08)

The United States safety board, the NTSB, investigated the Dali for twenty months. Their final report came out in November twenty twenty-five.
*[a thick report lands on a desk; the Ship Guy flips through it]*

And the first blackout came down to one wire.
*[zoom into the ship: electrical room → a cabinet → rows of wires]*

Inside an electrical cabinet, there's a strip of terminals where wires are clamped in. Each wire has a small plastic label wrapped around it, so engineers know which wire is which.
*[close-up: a terminal block, wires with little white labels]*

On one wire, that label had been put on in the wrong place when the ship was built. It stopped the wire from going all the way into its slot. So the wire was only half connected.
*[animation: the label blocks the wire; it sits half in]*

For years it held. Then that night, it lost contact, a breaker tripped, and the ship went dark.
*[the wire wiggles loose, a spark, the breaker flips, lights out]*

Finding it wasn't easy. That cabinet held hundreds of terminal blocks. Investigators tested the system for about a month before they took the panel apart and found the one loose wire. In the lab, it showed scrape marks and burn marks where electricity had been jumping across the gap.
*[the Ship Guy with a magnifying glass over a wall of wires; a tiny spark under the lens]*

And there was more. Some of the ship's main power switches were set to manual instead of automatic. The NTSB found that on automatic, the first blackout would have lasted about ten seconds. Instead, it lasted almost a minute.
*[two stopwatches side by side: "AUTO 10 s" and "MANUAL 58 s"]*

The second blackout had another cause. The crew had been using a pump meant for flushing and cleaning to feed fuel to the generators. When the power dropped, that pump stopped, and it didn't restart by itself. The generators ran out of fuel.
*[a pump labelled FLUSHING feeding the generators; it stops; the generators sputter]*

So a misplaced label, a switch set to manual, and the wrong pump, on the wrong night, three ship lengths from a bridge.
*[the Ship Guy holds up the tiny label again, and sighs]*

## 8. A port cut off (8:08–9:54)

Meanwhile, the Dali was stuck under the wreckage, with thousands of tonnes of bridge steel lying across its bow.
*[side view: the Dali with the bent truss draped over its front]*

And the river was blocked. The Port of Baltimore handles more cars and farm machines than any other port in the United States. Now it was cut off from the sea.
*[map: car carriers waiting outside, a red X on the channel; rows of cars on the quay]*

Thousands of dock workers, truck drivers and small businesses suddenly had almost no work coming in.
*[empty cranes on the quay; the Ship Guy with an empty clipboard]*

So the salvage teams started cutting. They brought in the largest floating crane on the East Coast, called the Chesapeake One Thousand, which can lift about a thousand tons at a time.
*[a giant floating crane lifting a huge chunk of truss; the Ship Guy tiny on the barge, looking up]*

And this crane has a strange past. It once helped the CIA secretly lift part of a sunken Soviet submarine from the bottom of the ocean.
*[the Ship Guy's eyes go wide; sunglasses drop onto his face; a "TOP SECRET" stamp]*

Piece by piece, they opened small channels so a few ships could get in and out, and then bigger ones.
*[the channel widening on the map, step by step]*

The first small channel opened just six days after the collapse, for tugs and barges. By late April, a deeper temporary channel let some of the biggest ships through again.
*[a tug squeezing through a narrow gap; then a big container ship following]*

Then, in May, they used small, carefully placed explosive charges to cut the biggest section of the bridge off the Dali's bow.
*[a controlled blast, the truss splits and drops away. The Ship Guy covering his ears]*

On the twentieth of May, the Dali finally floated free and was towed back into port. And on the tenth of June, the full shipping channel was open again. By then, crews had pulled an estimated fifty thousand tons of steel and concrete out of the river.
*[tugs pulling the Dali away; the channel clear, ships sailing in; a pile of steel labelled "≈50,000 t"]*

Most of the crew had stayed on the ship for weeks. Some were told to stay in Baltimore during the investigation, and a few were still there almost two years later.
*[the crew on deck, then a calendar flipping]*

And the Dali itself? After temporary repairs, it sailed to a shipyard in China, where its smashed bow was rebuilt. In January twenty twenty-five, about ten months after the collapse, it went back to work, carrying containers between Asia and Central America.
*[the Dali in dry dock with a new bow, then sailing out to sea; the Ship Guy watching from the quay, arms folded]*

## 9. What it costs, and what changes (9:54–10:51)

The ship's owners agreed to pay about one hundred and two million dollars for clearing the channel, without admitting they were responsible.
*[a cheque slides in: $102,000,000]*

When the Key Bridge was built in the nineteen-seventies, it cost about sixty million dollars. The new bridge will cost much more. The latest estimate is between four point three and five point two billion dollars, and it's not expected to open before late twenty thirty.
*[a price tag "$60 million (1977)", then a new bridge drawing builds up, a counter climbs to "$5.2 BILLION?", a calendar: 2030]*

It will have a longer main span, and much bigger protection around its supports.
*[the new bridge with huge barrier islands around its piers]*

And the investigation reached far beyond Baltimore. The NTSB asked the owners of sixty-eight other bridges across the United States, including the Golden Gate Bridge, to check how they'd survive a ship strike.
*[a US map with dots lighting up, the Golden Gate Bridge highlighted]*

A giant ship, a bridge that stood for almost fifty years, and a little plastic label in the wrong place. Sometimes the biggest disasters start with the smallest things.
*[the Ship Guy on a quiet riverbank at dawn, the tiny label in his hand]*

If you want to see another giant ship that got stuck in a very narrow place, and shut down a whole canal, that video is right here.
*[end screen: Ever Given video + subscribe]*

## Fact notes (please read before recording)

1. **Times:** sources differ by a minute or so (mayday 1:26/1:27, impact 1:28/1:29). The script says "twenty-five past one" for the
   blackout (NTSB: 1:24:59), "twenty-seven minutes past one" for the mayday, and "about one twenty-nine" for the impact.
2. **Speed at impact:** reports range from 6.4 to 8 knots. The script says "around seven knots".
3. **Deaths:** six road workers died, and two people were rescued (one from the river, one inspector from the structure).
   I left out names and nationalities on purpose (sources only confirm some of them). Tell me if you want a respectful line naming them.
4. **"A piece of plastic smaller than your finger":** the NTSB says improperly installed wire-label banding stopped the wire
   from fully seating in terminal block 381. "Smaller than your finger" is my plain-language description of a wire label.
5. **"For years it held":** the label was misplaced when the ship was built (2015), per NTSB reporting.
6. **"Well over a hundred thousand tonnes":** the Dali can carry 116,851 tonnes (deadweight). With the ship's own weight,
   ship + cargo is more than that, so "well over a hundred thousand" is safe even though it wasn't fully loaded to the limit.
7. **Pilots' orders:** news timelines report the pilot ordered the rudder hard over and the port anchor dropped. The NTSB also
   credits the pilots' quick actions. The script says the anchor couldn't stop it in time (NTSB: not enough time to recover).
8. **"Divers went in that same night… cold water":** divers searched that night; water temperature figures weren't confirmed,
   so the script just says "dark and cold" (late March, Chesapeake Bay).
9. **Sunshine Skyway:** most sources say 35 deaths (a few say 34 or 36). The 1991 vessel-collision guidance (AASHTO) was created
   in response to the NTSB's Skyway investigation, per the NTSB's own 2025 press release.
10. **Port numbers:** "more cars and farm machines than any other US port" and "~50,000 tons removed" are from AP reporting;
    the script says "an estimated fifty thousand tons".
11. **Crew:** all crew were allowed to leave the ship by June 2024; 11 senior crew had to stay in Baltimore, 8 were still there in
    March 2026. The script keeps it vague on purpose ("a few were still there almost two years later").
12. **Skyway survivor:** older reports say his truck landed on the ship's deck; newer reporting (St. Pete Catalyst, 40th
    anniversary) says it hit the hull and sank. The script uses the version both agree on: it hit the ship, sank, he was rescued by the crew.
13. **Artificial islands:** "on the newest bridges" is general (the new Sunshine Skyway and the planned new Key Bridge use large
    pier protection); not every new bridge has rock islands.
14. **Legal:** criminal cases against some people are still open (2026). The script does not name or blame any person.
