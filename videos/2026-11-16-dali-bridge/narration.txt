On board are twenty-one crew, and two local harbour pilots, experts who guide big ships through the harbour.

In a big port, ships don't find their own way in and out. Every harbour has its own bends, shallow spots and bridges, so local pilots come on board and give the steering orders until the ship reaches open water.

Ahead of it is the Francis Scott Key Bridge. It's about two point six kilometres long, and it carries a busy highway across the river.

And it has a famous name. In eighteen fourteen, Francis Scott Key watched a battle at Fort McHenry, just up this same river, and wrote the poem that became America's national anthem. Every summer, a red, white and blue buoy is placed in the water near the spot where he watched.

And on the bridge that night, a small road crew is doing a normal night-shift job: filling potholes.

Everything is normal. Until it isn't.

At twenty-five past one, when the ship is only about three ship lengths away from the bridge, every light on the Dali goes out.

To understand why that's so dangerous, you need to know one thing about big ships. A modern ship is basically a floating power station.

Generators make the electricity, and that electricity runs almost everything. The steering. The lights. The radios. And the pumps that keep the main engine alive.

The main engine is cooled by water, and that water is pushed around by electric pumps. So when the power dies, the cooling stops, and the main engine shuts itself down.

No engine means no propeller pushing water past the rudder. And without that flow, the rudder can barely steer.

So why not just stop? Because a ship this size can't stop like a car. There are no brakes. When it's fully loaded, the Dali and its cargo weigh well over a hundred thousand tonnes, and all of that keeps gliding forward.

Even at a walking pace, that much weight doesn't want to stop. Imagine trying to stop a sliding block of flats with your hands.

The pilots try everything they can. They order the rudder hard over to turn away, and they drop an anchor to drag the ship and slow it down.

But an anchor isn't a parking brake. It digs in and drags, and that takes time. Three ship lengths is not much time.

The crew get some power back within about a minute. And then, it goes out again.

At twenty-seven minutes past one, a mayday goes out on the radio. The ship has lost power, and it's heading for the bridge.

What happened next probably saved a lot of lives. The message reached the police, and officers at both ends of the bridge stopped the traffic.

They had about ninety seconds. And in that time, no more cars drove onto the bridge.

One officer even radioed that he would drive onto the bridge to warn the road crew. There wasn't enough time.

Later, the NTSB said the quick actions of the pilots, the dispatchers and the police prevented a much bigger loss of life.

But the road crew in the middle didn't get the warning in time.

At about one twenty-nine, the Dali, moving at around seven knots, a fast walking pace, hits one of the bridge's main supports.

The bridge comes down in about thirty seconds.

Two people were rescued from the water and the wreckage. Six road workers died.

Divers went into the river that same night. The water was dark and cold, and full of twisted steel and broken concrete.

The middle part of the Key Bridge was a continuous steel truss, one long piece resting on a few supports. Take away one of those supports, and the long piece can't hold itself up. It all goes together.

Big bridges over shipping lanes are protected by barriers around their supports, called dolphins. They're there to catch or push away a ship before it reaches the bridge.

On the newest bridges, the supports even sit on artificial islands of rock, so a ship runs aground long before it can touch the bridge.

But the Key Bridge was built in the nineteen-seventies. Ships have become much bigger since then, and its protection was far too small for a ship like the Dali.

Later, the investigators calculated that the risk of this bridge collapsing after a ship strike was almost thirty times higher than what's acceptable for a bridge this important. And nobody had ever checked.

And here's the frustrating part. America had already learned this lesson once, the hard way.

It's the ninth of May, nineteen eighty, in Tampa Bay, Florida. Early in the morning, a sudden storm hits the bay.

A cargo ship called the Summit Venture is heading for the Sunshine Skyway Bridge. In the storm, the crew can barely see, and the ship's radar fails.

The ship hits one of the bridge's supports. A long section of the bridge falls into the bay.

Cars and a bus drove off the broken edge in the storm. Thirty-five people died.

One man survived. His pickup truck fell with the road, hit the side of the ship, and sank. He swam back to the surface, and the ship's own crew pulled him out of the water.

That disaster changed the rules. After it, engineers in the United States created a way to calculate how likely a bridge is to be hit by a ship, and how strong its protection needs to be. The new Sunshine Skyway was built with huge protective barriers around its supports.

But those rules were made for new bridges. The Key Bridge had been built before them. And nobody went back to check it against the bigger and bigger ships coming through.

The United States safety board, the NTSB, investigated the Dali for twenty months. Their final report came out in November twenty twenty-five.

And the first blackout came down to one wire.

Inside an electrical cabinet, there's a strip of terminals where wires are clamped in. Each wire has a small plastic label wrapped around it, so engineers know which wire is which.

On one wire, that label had been put on in the wrong place when the ship was built. It stopped the wire from going all the way into its slot. So the wire was only half connected.

For years it held. Then that night, it lost contact, a breaker tripped, and the ship went dark.

Finding it wasn't easy. That cabinet held hundreds of terminal blocks. Investigators tested the system for about a month before they took the panel apart and found the one loose wire. In the lab, it showed scrape marks and burn marks where electricity had been jumping across the gap.

And there was more. Some of the ship's main power switches were set to manual instead of automatic. The NTSB found that on automatic, the first blackout would have lasted about ten seconds. Instead, it lasted almost a minute.

The second blackout had another cause. The crew had been using a pump meant for flushing and cleaning to feed fuel to the generators. When the power dropped, that pump stopped, and it didn't restart by itself. The generators ran out of fuel.

So a misplaced label, a switch set to manual, and the wrong pump, on the wrong night, three ship lengths from a bridge.

Meanwhile, the Dali was stuck under the wreckage, with thousands of tonnes of bridge steel lying across its bow.

And the river was blocked. The Port of Baltimore handles more cars and farm machines than any other port in the United States. Now it was cut off from the sea.

Thousands of dock workers, truck drivers and small businesses suddenly had almost no work coming in.

So the salvage teams started cutting. They brought in the largest floating crane on the East Coast, called the Chesapeake One Thousand, which can lift about a thousand tons at a time.

And this crane has a strange past. It once helped the CIA secretly lift part of a sunken Soviet submarine from the bottom of the ocean.

Piece by piece, they opened small channels so a few ships could get in and out, and then bigger ones.

The first small channel opened just six days after the collapse, for tugs and barges. By late April, a deeper temporary channel let some of the biggest ships through again.

Then, in May, they used small, carefully placed explosive charges to cut the biggest section of the bridge off the Dali's bow.

On the twentieth of May, the Dali finally floated free and was towed back into port. And on the tenth of June, the full shipping channel was open again. By then, crews had pulled an estimated fifty thousand tons of steel and concrete out of the river.

Most of the crew had stayed on the ship for weeks. Some were told to stay in Baltimore during the investigation, and a few were still there almost two years later.

And the Dali itself? After temporary repairs, it sailed to a shipyard in China, where its smashed bow was rebuilt. In January twenty twenty-five, about ten months after the collapse, it went back to work, carrying containers between Asia and Central America.
