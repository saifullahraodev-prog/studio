# Long-form #1: "Why the Titanic Didn't Have Enough Lifeboats". SCRIPT DRAFT (for approval)

16:9, about 8:30-9:30 when read at a natural pace (~1,400 words). The Inspector opens and closes;
the narrator talks to "you". *(Italics = what we see.)*

---

## 0. Cold open (~0:30)

**INSPECTOR:** It's two-oh-five in the morning. The deck under your feet is tilting. The band is still
playing somewhere behind you. And you're standing in line for a lifeboat.

**NARRATOR:** There are about seven hundred seats left on this whole ship. There are more than two thousand
of you. How did the most famous ship in the world end up like this, and why was all of it perfectly legal?

**INSPECTOR:** Let's go back to where it started.

## 1. The unsinkable ship (~1:15)

**NARRATOR:** It's 1911. You're a riveter in Belfast, at the Harland and Wolff shipyard. All day you hammer
red-hot rivets into steel plates. Your crew is one of many. This ship will need around three million of
them.

She's called Titanic. When she's finished she'll be the largest moving object ever built: more than
two hundred and sixty metres long, and over forty-six thousand tons.

*(the hull towers over the riveters)*

And she's clever. Her hull is split into sixteen watertight compartments. Flood one, two, even four of them,
and she'll still float. A shipbuilding magazine calls her "practically unsinkable." Most people quietly
drop the word "practically."

On the thirty-first of May, 1911, she slides into the water. A hundred thousand people come to watch.

## 2. The rule from 1894 (~1:30)

**NARRATOR:** Now you're in London, at the Board of Trade. It's your job to make sure ships carry enough
lifeboats. You open the rulebook.

*(the rulebook opens to the 1894 table)*

It was written in 1894. Back then, a really big ship was ten thousand tons. So the table simply stops
there: "ten thousand tons and upwards: sixteen boats."

Sixteen boats for a ship of ten thousand tons. Sixteen boats for a ship of forty-six thousand. The table
doesn't care.

**INSPECTOR:** Ships had grown four times bigger in eighteen years. The rule never grew with them.

**NARRATOR:** At the shipyard, one of Titanic's designers, Alexander Carlisle, had chosen davits, the cranes
that lower the boats, strong enough to carry many more. He later said there was room for as many as
sixty-four lifeboats.

She sails with twenty. That's already more than the law asks for. Twenty boats can carry about one
thousand one hundred and seventy-eight people. On her first voyage, she'll carry about two thousand two
hundred.

*(20 boats line up next to a crowd of 2,200)*

Nobody is breaking a rule. That's the problem.

## 3. Sunday, 14 April 1912 (~1:30)

**NARRATOR:** It's Sunday night in the North Atlantic. You're the wireless operator, squeezed into the radio
room with headphones on.

All day, other ships have been warning about ice. Some of the messages reach the bridge. Some don't. By
evening there have been around six.

*(telegraph slips pile up)*

But you're buried in passenger telegrams: greetings, travel plans, stock tips, all paid by the word. Near
eleven at night, a nearby ship, the Californian, breaks in loud and clear. They're stopped, surrounded by
ice.

You're tired, the signal is deafening, and you're trying to reach the shore station at Cape Race. So you
tap back: "Shut up. I am busy."

The Californian's operator goes to bed.

*(crow's nest, freezing, no binoculars)*

High above, two lookouts stand in the crow's nest in the freezing dark. There is no moon. The sea is
dead calm, so there are no waves breaking at the base of anything out there.

At eleven-forty, one of them sees it. He rings the bell three times and grabs the phone:
"Iceberg, right ahead!"

*(the iceberg slides along the side)*

The ship turns, but not enough. For about ten seconds, the ice scrapes along her side. Up on deck, some
passengers barely notice. A few play football with the chunks of ice.

## 4. Two hours forty minutes (~2:00)

**NARRATOR:** Below the waterline, the sea is pouring into five compartments. She was built to survive four.

*(water in the compartments, one after another)*

Her designer walks the flooded decks and does the math. Titanic is going to sink. He thinks she has
maybe an hour and a half.

Just after midnight, the order comes: uncover the boats. Women and children first.

But it's freezing, it's calm, and the ship still feels solid. Why would you climb into a little boat and
be lowered into the black ocean? Many people don't believe it's serious.

So the first boats leave half empty. One boat built for sixty-five leaves with about twenty-eight
people. Another, built for forty, leaves with twelve.

*(a lifeboat rows away, half empty)*

Around one-fifteen, the bow is going under and the mood changes. Now everyone wants a seat. Officers
form lines and push people back. On one side of the ship, it's women and children only.

*(the deck tilts, the crowd presses toward the boats)*

At two-oh-five, the last boat leaves. More than fifteen hundred people are still on board. The band
keeps playing.

At two-twenty, Titanic breaks apart and sinks.

*(quiet: the sea at night, the lights going out)*

The water is minus two degrees. Most of the people in it are gone within minutes. Only a handful of
lifeboats row back to look for survivors.

## 5. The ship that came (~1:00)

**NARRATOR:** Now you're on a smaller ship, the Carpathia, about fifty-eight miles away. Her wireless
operator heard the distress call almost by luck: he was just about to go to bed.

The captain, Arthur Rostron, turns her around and races north through the ice field in the dark, with
extra lookouts on every corner of the deck.

*(Carpathia ploughing through ice, sparks from her funnel)*

She arrives about an hour and a half after Titanic went down. As the sky gets lighter, you see the
lifeboats scattered across the sea, and icebergs all around them.

By morning, the Carpathia has picked up about seven hundred survivors. Everyone else is lost.

## 6. Who's to blame? (~1:15)

**NARRATOR:** Back on land, the questions start. Two investigations open: one by the United States Senate,
one by Britain.

*(the inquiry room; witnesses, papers, the rulebook on the table)*

They hear from lookouts, officers, passengers and designers. The radio operator's "I am busy." The
missing binoculars. The ship that was close enough to see Titanic's rockets but never came.

And then they look at the lifeboats.

**INSPECTOR:** Twenty boats. Rules required sixteen. Titanic carried more boats than the law asked for.

**NARRATOR:** That's the shock. The ship was legal. The rule was wrong.

## 7. The fix (~1:00)

**NARRATOR:** In 1914, nations meet in London and sign the first International Convention for the Safety of
Life at Sea. Sea-lohs, for short.

*(the treaty signed; boats for everyone)*

Lifeboats, and lifejackets, for every person on board. Radios with someone listening, day and night.
And an International Ice Patrol to watch the North Atlantic, still running more than a century later.

**INSPECTOR:** Every safety drill you've ever done on a ferry or a cruise ship traces back to one cold
night in April 1912.

And that's why, today, every ship must carry a lifeboat or life-raft seat for everyone on board.

### Careful wording
- "About seven hundred seats left" (cold open) is a narrative estimate for ~2:05 a.m. (most boats gone, the
  collapsibles left). Safer alternative if you prefer: "There aren't enough seats left on this ship."
- Lifeboat 7's count varies (19-29 in sources); script says "about twenty-eight".
- SOLAS 1914 never formally came into force (WWI), but its rules were adopted; the script says "sign".
