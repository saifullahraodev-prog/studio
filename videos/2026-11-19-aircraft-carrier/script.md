## 0. Hook: the hardest landing in the world

It's night. You're flying a fighter jet at around two hundred and forty kilometres an hour.
*[cockpit view at night: dark sea, a tiny lit strip far ahead]*

Somewhere down there in the dark is your runway. It's moving. It's rising and falling with the waves. And the patch of deck you have to hit is smaller than almost any runway on land.
*[the lit strip rising and falling; a tiny target box drawn on it]*

You can't slow down to land. You can't stop on your own. Something has to grab your plane and stop it for you, in about two seconds.
*[the Ship Guy in the cockpit, gripping the stick, sweating]*

And the moment your wheels hit the deck, you do something that sounds completely crazy. You push the engines to full power.
*[throttle slammed forward; engine roar]*

Why? To answer that, we need to understand how an aircraft carrier really works.

## 1. A runway that floats

The biggest aircraft carriers in the world belong to the United States Navy: the Nimitz class, and the newest one, the Gerald R. Ford.
*[side view of a supercarrier sailing; the Ship Guy waving from a small boat beside it, tiny]*

A Nimitz carrier is about three hundred and thirty metres long. Stand it on its end, and it's about as tall as the Eiffel Tower.
*[the carrier rotates upright beside the Eiffel Tower; the tops line up]*

Fully loaded, it weighs around a hundred thousand tonnes, and it can still sail at more than thirty knots.
*[the carrier speeding up, a wake behind it; a speed card "30+ KNOTS"]*

With its air wing on board, around five thousand people live and work on a Nimitz carrier. That's more than many small towns.
*[the hull turns see-through: rows of tiny bunks, a town sign "POP. ~5,000"]*

And it doesn't sail alone. Cruisers and destroyers guard it against missiles and submarines, and supply ships keep it stocked. Together they're called a carrier strike group, and the escorts bring thousands more people.
*[top-down: the carrier in the middle, escort ships around it, a supply ship alongside]*

But the whole point of this ship is its aircraft. An air wing usually has more than forty strike fighters, like the F/A-18 Super Hornet and the F-35C, plus the specialists: the E-2D Hawkeye with its giant radar dish, the EA-18G Growler that jams enemy radar, helicopters for submarine hunting and rescue, and transport aircraft that bring people, mail and parts.
*[jets pop onto the deck one by one with name tags; the Hawkeye's dish spins; a helicopter lowers a rescue line; a transport plane lands with a mailbag]*

The exact mix changes from ship to ship and mission to mission. But every one of those aircraft has the same problem. This runway is far too short.

## 2. Zero to flying speed in two seconds

On land, a fighter jet often uses well over a kilometre of runway. On a Nimitz carrier, the catapult gives it about ninety metres.
*[two strips side by side: a long land runway vs the short catapult track]*

So the carrier throws it into the air.
*[the Ship Guy pointing at a long slot in the deck]*

Under the deck runs a long track. The jet's nose wheel is attached to a shuttle that rides in that track.
*[cut-away under the deck: the shuttle in its track, the nose wheel locked on]*

The pilot pushes the engines to full power, but the jet doesn't move, because a holdback is holding it in place.
*[the jet straining, engines glowing; a metal bar at the back holding it]*

Behind it, a steel wall rises out of the deck. It's a jet blast deflector, and it stops the scorching exhaust from hitting the people and planes behind.
*[a deck plate tilting up behind the jet; the hot exhaust bouncing off it; the Ship Guy hiding behind it]*

The deck crew check everything. Then the catapult officer, nicknamed the shooter, gives the signal.
*[the shooter in a yellow jersey, a crouch, arm pointing down the deck]*

The holdback lets go, and the catapult fires. In about two seconds, the jet goes from standing still to flying speed, around two hundred and eighty kilometres an hour for a typical fighter.
*[whoosh: the jet streaks off the bow; a speed counter 0 → ~280 km/h in 2 s; the Ship Guy's cap blown off]*

On Nimitz carriers, that push comes from steam, made with heat from the ship's reactors. The Ford uses an electric system called EMALS. It stores energy in big spinning rotors between launches, then releases it through electronic power converters into a long electric motor that pulls the shuttle down the track. It's easier to fine-tune for heavy jets and light drones alike.
*[split screen: steam pistons on the left; on the right, spinning wheels charging up, then a flash of current racing along a track]*

The carrier usually helps too. It steers into the wind, so more air is already flowing over the wings.
*[the carrier turning into wind arrows; the flag on the mast standing straight]*

Launching is violent. But bringing that jet back is even harder.

## 3. Catching a jet with a wire

Let's go back to our night landing.
*[cockpit again, the deck lights getting closer]*

To stay on the right path down, the pilot watches a light on the side of the deck called the meatball. If the ball of light sits level with a row of green lights, you're on the glide path. Too high, and it rises. Too low, and it sinks.
*[the optical landing light: a yellow ball beside a row of green lights, sliding up and down]*

Beside the landing area stands a landing signal officer, an experienced pilot who watches every approach and talks the pilots in by radio.
*[an officer in a white jersey with a radio handset, at the edge of the deck]*

Across the landing area are thick steel cables, raised a little above the deck. Most Nimitz-class carriers have four. The Ford, and the two newest Nimitz ships, have three.
*[top-down: the cables across the deck, numbered 1 to 4]*

Under the tail of the jet hangs a hook. The pilot flies the plane firmly onto the deck so the hook can catch one of those wires. On a four-wire deck, pilots aim for the third, because it gives the safest margin from the back edge of the ship.
*[side view: the hook drops, skips, and catches wire 3]*

Then the wire pays out, pulling against huge machines below the deck that soak up the jet's energy. The system is built to stop a jet weighing more than twenty tonnes, coming in at around two hundred and forty kilometres an hour, in roughly a hundred metres. In about two seconds.
*[the jet stopping hard, the cable stretching; below deck, a big cylinder squeezing; the Ship Guy, riding on the wing, flying forward into a cartoon pile]*

Those cables take a terrible beating, so the crew inspect them constantly and replace them whenever wear and maintenance rules say so.
*[green-jersey crew checking a cable with a gauge, then rolling a new one out]*

## 4. Why full throttle?

So, why push to full power at the exact moment you land?
*[back to the cockpit: the throttle slammed forward]*

Because sometimes the hook misses every wire. That's called a bolter.
*[the hook bouncing over all the wires]*

If the engines were at low power, the jet could roll off the end of the deck into the sea. At full power, it simply takes off again, flies around, and tries one more time.
*[the jet roaring off the end of the deck and climbing back up; the Ship Guy sighing with relief]*

That's also why the landing area is angled, about nine degrees off to the side. A jet that misses flies off past the bow, instead of crashing into the planes parked at the front.
*[top-down: the angled landing strip drawn in; a jet missing and flying off to the side, safely past the parked jets]*

And if a jet can't land normally at all, say its hook is damaged, the crew can raise a giant net across the deck, called the barricade, and catch the plane in it.
*[a tall net raised across the deck; a jet rolling into it and stopping]*

## 5. The rainbow on the deck

All of this happens on a deck packed with jets, spinning engines and fuel. So how do they keep people safe?
*[top-down busy deck: jets moving, people everywhere]*

With colours. Everyone on the flight deck wears a coloured jersey that shows their job.
*[the Ship Guy holding up jerseys one by one, like a fashion show]*

Yellow are the officers and directors who control and move the aircraft, including the catapult and arresting-gear officers.
*[a yellow jersey guiding a jet with arm signals]*

Green run and maintain the catapults and arresting gear, along with many aircraft maintenance crews.
*[green jerseys at the catapult track]*

Purple handle the fuel. Everyone calls them grapes.
*[purple jerseys with a fuel hose; a cartoon bunch of grapes pops up]*

Red handle bombs and missiles, and crash and salvage.
*[red jerseys rolling a missile on a cart; a crash crew with a hose]*

Blue chain the jets down, chock the wheels, and drive the tractors and elevators.
*[blue jerseys with chains and chocks]*

Brown are the plane captains, each responsible for getting one jet ready.
*[a brown jersey checking a canopy]*

And white covers several safety jobs: landing signal officers, safety observers, inspectors and medical teams.
*[white jerseys with clipboards and a red cross]*

At a glance, anyone can see who's doing what.

High up on the island, the tower beside the deck, is primary flight control. That's where the Air Boss runs every aircraft on the deck and in the air close to the ship.
*[zoom up the island to a window full of screens; the Air Boss with a headset]*

During busy flying, launches and landings often run in waves called cycles. A common rhythm is roughly every hour and a half, with a dozen or more aircraft in each, but it changes with the mission. A busy airport, squeezed onto a moving deck, in the middle of the sea.
*[a clock face; jets launching from the front while others line up to land at the back]*

## 5b. The day it went wrong

The Navy didn't learn all of this on paper.
*[the Ship Guy, serious, at the edge of a quiet deck; no gags in this part]*

On the twenty-ninth of July, nineteen sixty-seven, the carrier USS Forrestal was preparing to launch aircraft off the coast of Vietnam.
*[an older carrier at sea, jets lined up at the back, crews working]*

On the crowded deck, an electrical malfunction fired a Zuni rocket from under an F-4B Phantom. It struck the external fuel tank of an A-4E Skyhawk across the deck. Fuel poured out and caught fire, and bombs on the burning aircraft began to explode.
*[a quiet, simple shot: a small flash at the back of the deck, then smoke rising; no gags]*

One of the first men to run toward the fire was Chief Gerald Farrier, with a portable extinguisher. He was killed in the first explosion.
*[a single silhouette walking toward the smoke; the frame holds]*

One hundred and thirty-four men were killed. One hundred and sixty-one were injured. Twenty-one aircraft were destroyed.
*[a dark sky, the carrier far away with a column of smoke; a long pause on silence]*

The fire changed the Navy. It brought back firefighting training for every sailor, not just specialist teams, and reshaped how carriers handle fires on deck. Today, sailors train at a firefighting school named after Chief Farrier.
*[red jerseys in a training drill, foam spraying; a plain sign on a building: "FARRIER FIREFIGHTING SCHOOL"]*

On a ship, there's no fire brigade coming. The fire brigade is everyone on board.
*[the Ship Guy, in a red jersey, holding a hose with the crew; quiet]*

## 6. The garage below

Back to the flying. Not every aircraft fits on the flight deck at once.
*[the top deck crowded, jets packed wingtip to wingtip]*

Below it is a huge garage called the hangar bay, where jets are repaired and stored.
*[cut-away: the hangar bay below, jets with panels open, mechanics]*

To move jets between the two, there are giant elevators on the sides of the ship. A Nimitz carrier has four, and each one can lift a fighter from the hangar to the flight deck in seconds.
*[a side elevator platform rising with a jet on it; the Ship Guy riding up on it, waving]*

Separate weapons elevators bring bombs and missiles up from magazines deep inside the hull.
*[a weapons lift rising from deep below, red jerseys waiting]*

## 7. The nuclear heart

So what powers the catapults, the elevators, and a town of five thousand people?
*[the Ship Guy at a whiteboard: "POWER?"]*

Two nuclear reactors.
*[cut-away: two glowing reactor cores deep in the hull]*

They heat water into steam. The steam spins turbines that turn the propellers and make electricity for the whole ship.
*[animated: heat → steam → spinning turbine → propeller and lights]*

A Nimitz carrier runs for more than twenty years on its first load of fuel. Halfway through its life, it gets refuelled once, in a huge overhaul that takes several years.
*[a calendar flying through the years; the carrier in a dry dock with cranes]*

The Ford's newer reactors generate much more electricity. The Navy says nearly three times the capacity of earlier carriers. That's what makes the electric catapults possible.
*[two power bars: Nimitz vs Ford; the Ford bar shooting up]*

Nuclear power means the carrier never stops for its own fuel. But the jets still burn jet fuel, and lots of it. So supply ships regularly come alongside at sea, pumping over aviation fuel and passing across food, parts and ammunition.
*[a supply ship sailing alongside, hoses and cables between them; crates swinging across; purple jerseys giving a thumbs up]*

## 8. Feeding a floating town

Now think about the people who keep those flights going, far out at sea for months.
*[the Ship Guy in a chef's hat in a giant galley]*

A Nimitz carrier's galleys can serve around eighteen thousand meals a day.
*[a conveyor of trays; a counter climbing to 18,000]*

The water? Distillers turn seawater into about four hundred thousand gallons of fresh water a day. That's around one and a half million litres.
*[sea water flowing in, steam, clean water coming out; a counter climbing]*

Most sailors sleep in stacked bunks, in rooms shared with dozens of others, with a small locker for everything they own.
*[a cut-away bunk room, three bunks high; the Ship Guy squeezing into the top one and bumping his head]*

There are gyms, a post office, a medical department with an operating room, and even a barber shop.
*[quick cuts: a gym, a mailbag, a hospital bed, a barber's chair with the Ship Guy getting a haircut]*

## 9. The newest giant

The newest carrier, the USS Gerald R. Ford, was commissioned in twenty seventeen, at a cost of about thirteen billion dollars.
*[the Ford sailing, a ribbon-cutting; a giant price tag: ~$13 BILLION]*

It has the electric catapults, new arresting gear, three elevators instead of four, and a smaller island moved further back to free up deck space.
*[a side-by-side: Nimitz vs Ford, the new parts lighting up]*

All of it is meant to launch and recover more aircraft. The Navy's design requirement is a hundred and sixty flights a day for weeks at a time, and up to two hundred and seventy in a short surge. Those are targets, not everyday numbers, and the Pentagon's own testers have questioned whether the ship can reach them.
*[a counter 160 → 270, then a question mark stamped on it]*

## 10. Back to the landing

So let's land that jet one more time.
*[night again: the cockpit, the deck lights]*

The ship has turned into the wind. You keep the meatball level with the green lights. The landing signal officer says you're looking good.
*[the meatball centred; a radio crackle]*

The wheels slam down. You push to full power, just in case. Your hook catches the third wire.
*[slam; throttle forward; the hook grabs the wire]*

And in about two seconds, your jet is standing still, on a floating runway, in the middle of the ocean.
*[the jet stopped; a yellow jersey already waving it out of the landing area; the Ship Guy giving a thumbs up]*

That's how an aircraft carrier works: a floating airport, a power station and a town, built around one job. Throw jets into the sky, and catch them again.
*[the carrier sailing into the sunrise]*

If you want to see what this ship costs compared to every other kind of ship, from a small fishing boat to the thirteen-billion-dollar Ford, that video is right here.
*[end screen: the ship-cost video + subscribe]*

## Fact notes (please read before recording)

1. **Landing speed / weight:** "around 240 km/h" (≈130 knots) is a typical approach speed. The "more than twenty tonnes at 240 km/h
   in ~100 m" line describes what the **arresting gear is built to handle** (rated 50,000 lb at 130 kn), not a typical jet's weight.
2. **Eiffel Tower:** carrier 333 m, tower 330 m with antennas (300 m without). "About as tall" is the honest wording (see revision notes).
3. **Crew ~5,000:** Nimitz class with the air wing embarked (Navy fact file). The Ford is designed for fewer people; not used for the Ford.
4. **Wires:** four on most Nimitz ships; three on Ronald Reagan, George H.W. Bush and the Ford class. "Aim for the third" is said only for four-wire decks.
5. **Air wing / strike group / cycles:** all said as typical and variable, no fixed numbers.
6. **Forrestal:** Navy numbers (134 / 161 / 21). Serious tone, no gags, held pauses. Chief Farrier detail from NHHC and All Hands.
7. **Ford sorties:** design requirements; DOT&E has questioned them. **Cost:** ~$13B per CRS (often called the most expensive warship
   ever built; the script no longer claims that outright).
8. Length: about 1,850 words → ~10:15 (≈9:50 after pause trimming). Exact length after recording.
