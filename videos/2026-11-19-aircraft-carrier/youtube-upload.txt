It's night. You're landing a fighter jet on a runway that moves, rises and falls with the waves, and you have about two seconds to stop. And the moment your wheels hit the deck, you push the engines to full power. Why?

This is how a US Navy aircraft carrier really works: the catapults that throw jets into the sky, the wires that catch them, the crews in coloured jerseys who keep it all safe, the fire that changed the Navy, the reactors that power it, and the floating town of about five thousand people who live on board.

📊 KEY FACTS
• Nimitz class: about 333 m long, about 100,000 tonnes fully loaded, 30+ knots, two nuclear reactors
• About 5,000 people on board with the air wing embarked (Nimitz class)
• Air wing: usually 40+ strike fighters, plus radar, electronic-attack, helicopter and transport aircraft (varies by deployment)
• Arresting gear: built to stop a jet of more than 20 tonnes from about 240 km/h in roughly 100 m, in about 2 seconds
• Most Nimitz carriers have 4 wires; the Ford and the two newest Nimitz ships have 3
• USS Forrestal, 29 July 1967: 134 killed, 161 injured, 21 aircraft destroyed
• Ford: commissioned 2017, about $13 billion; the Navy says nearly three times the electrical capacity of earlier carriers
• Ford sortie numbers (160 a day / 270 surge) are design requirements, not everyday numbers

We remember the 134 men who died aboard USS Forrestal.
