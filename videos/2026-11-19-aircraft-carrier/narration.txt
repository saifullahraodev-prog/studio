It's night. You're flying a twenty-three-tonne fighter jet at two hundred and forty kilometres an hour.

Somewhere down there in the dark is your runway. It's moving. It's going up and down with the waves. And it's shorter than a football pitch.

You can't slow down to land. You can't stop on your own. Something has to grab your plane and stop it for you, in about two seconds.

And the moment your wheels hit the deck, you do something that sounds completely crazy. You push the engines to full power.

Why? To answer that, we need to understand how an aircraft carrier really works.

The biggest aircraft carriers in the world belong to the United States Navy. The Nimitz class, and the newest one, the Gerald R. Ford.

They're about three hundred and thirty metres long. Stand one on its end, and it's taller than the Eiffel Tower.

A fully loaded one weighs about a hundred thousand tonnes. And it carries up to ninety aircraft.

On a Nimitz-class carrier, around five thousand people live on board when the air wing is embarked. That's more people than many small towns.

And it can sail at more than thirty knots. That's over fifty-five kilometres an hour, for a ship the size of a skyscraper.

And it never sails alone. A carrier usually travels with a whole team of ships called a carrier strike group: cruisers and destroyers around it to shoot down missiles and hunt submarines, sometimes a submarine of its own hiding underneath, and supply ships bringing fuel, food and ammunition. Around seven and a half thousand people in the whole group.

So what are those ninety aircraft? Most are strike fighters: F/A-18 Super Hornets, and the newer F-35C stealth jets.

Then there are the specialists. The E-2D Hawkeye carries a giant radar dish on its back, so the group can see far beyond the horizon. The EA-18G Growler jams enemy radars. And helicopters hunt submarines and pick up anyone who ends up in the water.

A typical air wing today has around forty-four strike fighters, plus the radar planes, the jammers and the helicopters.

But a runway at sea has a problem that no airport on land has. It's far too short.

A fighter jet on land needs a runway that's often more than a kilometre long. On a carrier, the jet gets about ninety metres.

So the carrier throws it into the air. With a catapult.

Under the deck runs a long track. The jet's nose wheel is hooked onto a shuttle that rides in that track.

The pilot pushes the engines to full power. But the jet doesn't move yet, because a holdback bar is holding it in place.

Behind the jet, a big steel wall rises out of the deck. It's called a jet blast deflector, and it stops the scorching exhaust from blasting the people and planes behind it.

The deck crew check everything. Then the catapult officer, called the shooter, gives the signal.

The holdback bar releases, and the catapult fires. In about two seconds, the jet goes from standing still to around two hundred and eighty kilometres an hour.

On the Nimitz carriers, that push comes from steam, made with heat from the ship's nuclear reactors. On the new Ford, it's electric: a system called EMALS uses electromagnets to pull the shuttle down the track. Smoother, easier to fine-tune, and better for both very heavy and very light aircraft.

And the carrier helps too. It usually turns into the wind before a launch, so more air is already rushing over the wings.

Launching is violent. But landing is even harder.

Let's go back to our night landing.

To find the right path down, the pilot watches a light on the side of the deck called the meatball. If the ball of light is centred between two rows of green lights, you're on the right glide path. Too high, and it moves up. Too low, and it drops.

On the deck, a landing signal officer, an experienced pilot, watches every approach and talks the pilots down by radio.

Across the landing area are thick steel cables, stretched a little above the deck. Nimitz-class ships have four. The Ford has three.

Under the back of the jet hangs a hook. The pilot's job is to slam the plane down so the hook catches one of those wires. Pilots aim for the third wire on a four-wire carrier, because the first one is closest to the back edge of the ship.

Then the wire pays out, pulling against huge machines below the deck. A twenty-three-tonne jet coming in at about two hundred and forty kilometres an hour is stopped in roughly a hundred metres. In about two seconds.

The wires take such a beating that they're replaced after every hundred and twenty-five landings.

So, why do pilots push to full power at the exact moment they land?

Because sometimes the hook misses every wire. That's called a bolter.

If that happens and the engines are at low power, the jet would roll off the end of the deck into the sea. With full power, it simply takes off again, goes around, and tries one more time.

That's also why the landing area is angled. On modern carriers, it points about nine degrees off to the side. So a jet that misses flies off to the side, instead of crashing into the planes parked at the front.

And if a jet can't land normally at all, because its hook is damaged for example, the crew can stretch a giant net across the deck called the barricade, and catch the plane in it.

All of this happens on a deck packed with jets, engines and fuel. So how does nobody get hurt?

With colours. Everyone on the flight deck wears a coloured jersey that shows their job.

Yellow are the directors who move every aircraft around the deck with hand signals.

Green run the catapults and the arresting wires.

Purple handle the jet fuel. Everyone calls them grapes.

Red handle bombs and missiles, and fight fires.

Blue chain the jets down, put chocks under the wheels, and drive the tractors and elevators.

Brown are the plane captains, who look after one jet each like it's their own car.

And white are safety, medical, and the landing signal officers.

So at a glance, anyone can see who's doing what. On a deck this dangerous, that saves lives.

And all of it is run from the island, the tall tower on the side of the deck. Near the top is a room called primary flight control. That's where the Air Boss sits, in charge of every aircraft on the deck and in the air around the ship.

Launches and landings happen in waves called cycles. Roughly every hour and a half, a group of about twelve to twenty aircraft launches, and the group that went out earlier comes back to land. It's a busy airport, squeezed onto a moving parking lot, in the middle of the sea.

Why so careful? Because the Navy learned the hard way what can happen on a crowded flight deck.

On the twenty-ninth of July, nineteen sixty-seven, the carrier USS Forrestal was getting ready to launch aircraft off the coast of Vietnam.

On the deck, a rocket under the wing of one jet fired by accident. It hit another plane's fuel tank. Burning jet fuel spread across the deck, and the bombs loaded on the planes began to explode.

A hundred and thirty-four sailors and aviators were killed. Twenty-one aircraft were destroyed.

After the Forrestal fire, the Navy changed the way it trains sailors to fight fires at sea. Today, sailors on every carrier drill for a flight deck fire again and again, because on a ship, the fire brigade is you.

Of course, ninety aircraft don't all fit on the top deck at once.

Under the flight deck is a huge garage called the hangar bay, where jets are repaired and stored.

To move jets between the two, there are giant elevators on the sides of the ship. A Nimitz carrier has four of them, and each one can lift fighter jets from the hangar up to the flight deck in seconds.

There are separate elevators for bombs and missiles too, which come up from storage rooms deep inside the ship.

So what powers a hundred-thousand-tonne ship, a catapult, and a town of five thousand people?

Two nuclear reactors.

The reactors heat water into steam. The steam spins turbines that turn the propellers, and make electricity for the whole ship.

On a Nimitz-class carrier, the reactors run for more than twenty years without new fuel. In its whole life, the ship is refuelled only once, halfway through, in a huge overhaul that can take around four years.

The Ford's new reactors make far more electricity, about three times as much as the older design sets aside for its systems. That's what lets it run electric catapults instead of steam.

And because the reactors don't need oil, the carrier itself never has to stop for fuel. All its tanks can carry fuel for the jets instead.

Now think about the people. Five thousand of them, far out at sea, for months.

A Nimitz carrier's galleys can serve around eighteen thousand meals a day.

And all that water? The ship makes it from the sea. Its distillers turn seawater into about four hundred thousand gallons of fresh water every day. That's around one and a half million litres.

Most sailors sleep in stacked bunks, in rooms shared with dozens of other people, each with a small locker for everything they own.

There are gyms, a post office, a hospital with operating rooms, and even a barber shop.

The newest carrier, the USS Gerald R. Ford, was commissioned in twenty seventeen.

It cost about thirteen billion dollars, making it the most expensive warship ever built.

It has the electric catapult, new electric arresting gear, a smaller control tower moved further back, and new weapons elevators. The goal was to launch more flights every day than the older carriers: a hundred and sixty a day, and up to two hundred and seventy in an emergency.
