It's Friday the thirteenth of January, twenty twelve. Nine forty-five at night. A cruise ship is sailing along the coast of Italy with more than four thousand people on board. Most of the passengers are at dinner.

Then the whole ship shudders. The lights go out. And somewhere below the waterline, the sea starts pouring in.

Thirty-two people never made it home. And the wreck left behind was so big, so heavy, and in such an awkward spot, that getting it out became one of the most expensive salvage jobs in history.

So how did a modern cruise ship hit a rock on a calm night? And how do you lift a sunken ship that's almost three hundred metres long? Let's go back to that evening.

The ship was called the Costa Concordia. Built in Italy in two thousand and six, she was about two hundred and ninety metres long, with room for more than three thousand seven hundred passengers. Pools, theatres, restaurants, a spa. Basically a floating city.

That night, she had just left the port of Civitavecchia, near Rome, on a week-long cruise around the Mediterranean. On board were three thousand two hundred and six passengers and one thousand and twenty-three crew. The weather was good. The sea was calm.

The next stop was Savona, in the north. And the route went past a small island called Giglio.

Now, a big ship normally passes an island like that with plenty of room to spare. But that night, the ship was taken much closer to the coast than usual. The plan was to pass about half a mile from the shore. At night. In an area with very few lights.

And she was moving fast. About sixteen knots. That's roughly thirty kilometres an hour, for a ship that weighs as much as a small town.

Italy's official investigators later went through everything that happened on the bridge. They wrote that the close pass had been planned on a chart that wasn't detailed enough for it. That people who weren't part of the watch were on the bridge. And that there was a phone call that had nothing to do with navigation.

They also said something about the officers on duty. Nobody seemed to push the captain to slow down or turn away in time.

By the time the danger was clear, it was too late.

At nine forty-five and seven seconds, the Costa Concordia hit a group of rocks off Giglio called the Scole.

The rock tore a gash in the left side of the hull, fifty-three metres long, below the waterline.

To understand why that was so bad, you need to know how a ship stays afloat when it's damaged. The hull is split into watertight compartments, like an ice cube tray. If one or two flood, the ship should still float.

The Concordia's gash opened five of them at once. And those five held the heart of the ship. The engine room. The propulsion motors. All six of the main generators.

So within moments, the ship lost her engines and her power. The emergency generator kept cutting in and out. And the rudder was stuck, turned hard to one side.

A ship with more than four thousand people on board was now drifting in the dark, with water rushing in.

And here's the part that still shocks people. For more than an hour, the ship did not order everyone to leave.

At first, the coast guard didn't even hear it from the ship. Someone on shore raised the alarm, and the rescue started at sixteen minutes past ten.

By twenty-seven minutes past ten, the water had reached the deck that's supposed to stay dry, the top of the watertight compartments. And once that happens, the sea can spill from one compartment into the next, like water tipping over the top of an ice cube tray.

The official distress call only came at twenty-two thirty-eight. And the order to abandon ship came at five to eleven. That's more than an hour after the rocks. And before it, the general emergency alarm that should have warned everyone in time was not sounded straight away.

By then, the ship had already started to lean.

There was one piece of luck. With the rudder jammed, the drifting ship turned around by herself and drifted back toward the island. At around eleven o'clock, she ran aground on a rock shelf near Giglio's little harbour, just off the shore.

If she had sunk in open water, it could have been much worse.

The first lifeboats went down at five to eleven, and reached the shore a quarter of an hour later. But the ship kept leaning. Forty degrees around midnight. Later, almost eighty.

On a ship leaning that far, lifeboats on the high side can't be lowered at all. Corridors become walls. Stairs become ladders. People climbed down rope ladders on the side of the hull, waited on deck, or jumped into the sea and swam for the rocks.

Twenty-five patrol boats, fourteen other vessels, four tugs and eight helicopters joined the rescue. The people of Giglio opened their homes, their churches and their school to the survivors.

And the captain? He left the ship while hundreds of people were still on board. A coast guard officer famously ordered him, by phone, to go back. He didn't.

The first rescue finished at seventeen minutes past six the next morning. Four thousand one hundred and ninety-four people were saved that night. Three more were found alive two days later.

But thirty-two people died, passengers and crew.

Then came a different kind of problem.

The Concordia was now lying on her side, on a sloping rock shelf, right next to a small island in a protected marine area. More than a hundred thousand tonnes of ship, full of fuel, food, furniture and everything the passengers had left behind.

First, salvors pumped out the fuel. More than two thousand cubic metres of oil were removed in the first months, before it could leak into the sea.

Then came the big question. What do you do with the ship?

The easy answer would have been to cut her up right there, piece by piece. But that would have taken years, right next to the island, with pieces of wreck and dirt falling into the sea.

So the owners chose the harder plan. Turn the whole ship upright, float her, and tow her away in one piece.

The job went to two companies working together. Titan Salvage, from the United States, and Micoperi, from Italy. Hundreds of workers and divers from all over the world.

And the trick they used has a name. Parbuckling.

Parbuckling means rolling something upright by pulling it around its own side. Like rolling a barrel up a ramp with ropes. Except this barrel was a cruise ship.

But first, they had to stop her from sliding off the rock shelf into deep water. So divers filled huge bags with cement and packed them under the hull. And they drilled into the granite seabed and built six giant steel platforms next to the ship, to catch her when she turned.

Then they welded enormous steel boxes, called sponsons, onto the side that was sticking out of the water. Some of them were taller than a house.

And they ran thick chains under the hull, linked to steel cables and powerful jacks, all anchored to the seabed.

On the sixteenth of September twenty thirteen, at nine in the morning, they started pulling.

At first, nothing moved. The ship had been lying in the same spot for twenty months, and she was stuck on the rocks. The jacks pulled harder and harder, until, with a groan of steel, she finally came free.

Then, very slowly, she started to turn. All day. All night.

Partway up, the engineers let seawater flood into the steel boxes. Now the weight of that water did the rest of the work, pulling the ship the rest of the way round.

At four in the morning on the seventeenth of September, about nineteen hours after they started, the Costa Concordia was sitting upright on the platforms.

The side that had been underwater for twenty months was brown, crushed and twisted.

But she still couldn't float. So the salvors welded more sponsons onto the other side. Thirty in total. Then, in July twenty fourteen, they pushed the water out of them with compressed air, and the ship slowly rose off the platforms.

On the twenty-third of July, tugs started towing her north. About two hundred nautical miles, in four days, at walking speed. On the twenty-seventh, she arrived in Genoa.

There, it took around three years to take her apart. About ninety per cent of the ship was recycled.

So how much did all of this cost?

Costa's chief executive said the whole operation, from the reef to Genoa, would cost around one and a half billion euros. Roughly two billion dollars. Much more than it cost to build the ship in the first place.

And it wasn't only money. A diver working on the salvage also died.

The captain, Francesco Schettino, was convicted of manslaughter, of causing the shipwreck, and of abandoning the ship. He was sentenced to sixteen years in prison. In May twenty seventeen, Italy's highest court upheld that sentence, and he went to jail.

The official report didn't only blame one man, though. It said the flooding was far beyond what the ship was designed to survive. That the rules at the time didn't require the ship to have sensors to detect flooding. And that the bridge team didn't work as a team.

So let's go back to that night.

Nine forty-five. A calm sea. A ship that was sailing too close, too fast, in the dark.

One rock. A fifty-three metre gash. An hour before anyone was told to leave.

Thirty-two people lost. Two years of engineering to lift what was left. And around two billion dollars.

All because of a few minutes on the bridge.

And if you want to see another ship that lost all her power at the worst possible moment, that story is right here.
